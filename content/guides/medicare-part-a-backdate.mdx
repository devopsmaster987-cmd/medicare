## The six-month rule

Part A has a retroactive enrollment rule. If you delay enrollment and sign up later, the coverage
can start up to six months before the month you apply. The rule helps people who kept working and
kept employer coverage, because it can close a coverage gap.

The backdate is not unlimited. It cannot go back before the month you first became eligible for
Medicare. If you were first eligible at 65 and apply at 67, the backdate is measured from the
application date, not from age 65, and it still stops at the eligibility limit.

The rule applies to Part A. Part B does not use the same retroactive start. That difference is why a
person can have Part A for earlier months while Part B begins later. The premium notices can arrive
with different start dates, and that is normal.

## Part A backdate 6 months

Six months is the maximum, not a promise. The exact start date depends on the application date, the
eligibility date, and the Social Security rules for the situation. A person who applies in June may
see Part A start in December of the prior year if the eligibility date allows it.

The backdate is why the HSA stop rule exists. If Part A starts in December, the person was entitled
to Medicare in December, even if the card arrived in June. HSA contributions for December through
the enrollment months can be excess contributions.

The backdate can also create six months of Part A premiums for someone who is not premium-free. A
person with fewer than 30 work credits pays the full Part A premium. Six months at that rate can be
a meaningful bill, and it may be deducted from a benefit or billed directly.

## Who should check the date

Check the date if you worked past 65, if you had employer coverage and delayed Medicare, if you are
signing up during a Special Enrollment Period, or if you are helping a parent enroll. The date is
also important if you have an HSA, because the tax rules follow the Medicare entitlement date.

Check the date before you stop employer coverage. A backdated Part A can overlap with the employer
plan, and the coordination rules depend on the employer size and the type of coverage. The
[Medicare vs ACA](/calculators/medicare-vs-aca/) calculator walks through the 20-employee rule and
the premium-free Part A test.

Check the date before you make an HSA contribution for the year. A contribution that looks fine in
January can become excess if Part A is backdated to December.

## How the date changes the premium

For premium-free Part A, the backdate usually does not create a Part A premium. The person still
gets the coverage, and the HSA rule still applies. A zero premium does not mean the months are
ignored.

For reduced or full Part A, the backdate can create a bill for the retroactive months. The monthly
amounts for {year} are {formatMoney(reducedPartA)} for 30 to 39 work credits and{' '}
{formatMoney(fullPartA)} for fewer than 30 credits. Six months at the full rate is{' '}
{formatMoney(sixMonthsFull)}. Six months at the reduced rate is {formatMoney(sixMonthsReduced)}.

The bill can be paid in a lump sum or through a deduction, depending on the SSA process. Ask for the
amount in writing before you agree to a payment method. The backdate is a coverage decision, and the
bill is the consequence.

## Worked example

A person worked past 65, kept employer coverage, and applies for Medicare in June. Social Security
approves a Part A start date in December of the prior year, the earliest date the six-month rule
allows. The person has 40 or more work credits, so Part A is premium-free. No Part A premium is due
for the backdated months.

The same person contributed to an HSA from January through May. Because Part A starts in December,
those contributions fall inside the Medicare entitlement period. They can be excess contributions
even though the card had not arrived. The fix is to remove the excess and earnings, not to wait.

If the person had fewer than 30 work credits, the same December start could create a Part A bill of
{formatMoney(sixMonthsFull)} for six months at the full rate. The coverage would be the same, but the
cost would be different.

## What to ask SSA

Ask these questions before you enroll:

- What is the Part A effective date, and how was it calculated?
- Does the six-month backdate apply to my situation?
- Will the backdate create a Part A premium, and how much is it?
- Does the start date affect my HSA eligibility?
- When will Part B start, and is it different from Part A?

Write down the answers and compare them with the notice. If the notice shows a different date, ask
for an explanation before you pay a premium bill.

## How the date interacts with Part B

Part B does not use the same six-month retroactive rule. A person can have Part A for earlier months
and Part B starting later. The two notices can show different dates, and the premium bill can look
inconsistent until both are read together.

Part B has its own enrollment periods and its own late-enrollment penalty. The penalty is based on
how long a person went without Part B after eligibility, with exceptions for creditable employer
coverage and certain other situations. The Part A backdate does not automatically fix a Part B delay.

If you are working past 65 and the employer plan is primary, the Part B decision may be delayed on
purpose. Read the [Medicare vs ACA](/calculators/medicare-vs-aca/) calculator and the
[Part B late enrollment penalty](/calculators/part-b-late-enrollment-penalty/) calculator before you
choose a start date.

## A checklist before you apply

Confirm the first month you were eligible for Medicare. Confirm the employer coverage dates and
whether the coverage is active or retiree coverage. Ask what Part A effective date SSA will use.
Ask whether the date creates a premium. Ask how the date affects any HSA.

Gather the work history, the employer coverage letter, and the HSA statements. If a spouse or
dependent is involved, gather the same documents for that person. Keep the application confirmation.

When the notice arrives, compare the effective date with the answers you wrote down. If the date is
different, ask for the calculation before you pay a bill. A short written question is easier to
track than a phone call.

## Part A backdate effective date

The effective date is the date Part A coverage begins, not the date the application is approved.
When a person signs up after delaying enrollment, the backdate can move the start date up to six
months before the application. It cannot move earlier than the date the person first became
eligible.

That rule can produce a date that is earlier than expected. A person who applies in June may have
Part A begin in January, or earlier, depending on the first eligible date. The exact date should be
confirmed in writing because it affects premiums, HSA contributions, and the start of coverage.

The backdate is not automatic in every situation. The enrollment rules and the reason for the delay
matter. A person who delayed because of active employer coverage may have a different effective date
than a person who simply missed the initial window. The application and the evidence should match
the reason for the delay.

If the date is wrong, ask Social Security to correct it before relying on it for other decisions.
A wrong date can create an HSA excess contribution or a premium surprise. Keep the enrollment
notice, the card, and any letter that shows the effective date.

The date also matters for the Part B special enrollment period. Part B and Part A can have different
start dates, and the eight-month window after employment or coverage ends is separate from the Part
A backdate. Check both dates rather than assuming one controls the other.

 A written effective date is the record to keep. If a later notice shows a different date, ask for the correction before the next premium or tax filing depends on it.

## What the backdate cannot guarantee

1. The six-month backdate is a maximum, not a guaranteed start date. Eligibility and application
   timing control the result.
2. A premium-free Part A backdate still affects HSA eligibility. The absence of a premium does not
   mean the coverage is ignored.
3. This page is guidance, not a benefits determination. Confirm the effective date with Social
   Security and keep the notice.
