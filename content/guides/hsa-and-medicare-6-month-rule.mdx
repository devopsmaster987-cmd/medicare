## Why the six-month window exists

Medicare Part A can be backdated by up to six months when someone signs up after delaying coverage.
The backdate is a benefit for people who worked past 65 or missed an enrollment window. It also
creates a trap for an HSA.

You cannot contribute to an HSA for any month after you are entitled to Medicare. If Part A is
backdated, you were entitled to Medicare during those earlier months even though the paperwork came
later. Contributions made for those months are excess contributions, and the tax rules apply.

That is why the safe rule is to stop contributions six months before the expected Part A start. If
you enroll earlier, the stop date moves earlier too. The date to watch is the effective date on the
Medicare record, not the day the application was signed.

## When contributions must stop

Start with the date Part A will begin. Count back six months. Stop HSA contributions before that
date. If Part A starts on the first day of a month, the stop date is the first day of the month six
months earlier.

For example, if Part A starts on June 1, the six-month window reaches back to December 1 of the
prior year. Contributions for December through May can be excess because Medicare coverage is
backdated to December. The exact result depends on the effective date and the months involved, so
check the Medicare record.

Do not wait for the Medicare card to arrive. By then the backdated months may already contain
contributions. If you are close to 65 and still working, ask your employer and your HSA custodian
about the stop date before the birthday month.

## Can I contribute to an HSA after 65

Age alone does not end HSA eligibility. You can contribute after 65 if you are still enrolled in a
qualifying high-deductible health plan, you are not enrolled in Medicare, and you have no other
disqualifying coverage. The tax deduction does not disappear at 65.

Enrollment in Medicare does end HSA eligibility. That includes Part A, even premium-free Part A.
Once you are entitled to Medicare, you cannot contribute for that month or later months. The
six-month rule is the practical consequence of that rule when Part A is backdated.

If you are still working at 65 and have employer coverage, the timing depends on the employer plan
and the Medicare enrollment decision. Read the [Medicare vs ACA](/calculators/medicare-vs-aca/)
calculator and talk to the employer benefits office before you stop or start anything.

## What happens to an excess contribution

An excess contribution is money that went into the HSA for a month when you were not eligible. The
tax rules generally impose a 6 percent excise tax on the excess for each year it remains in the
account. The fix is usually to remove the excess and the earnings on it before the tax filing
deadline.

Do not simply spend the extra money. Removing the contribution is not the same as taking a
distribution. Ask the HSA custodian for a correction of excess contributions, and report it the way
the IRS instructions require.

If the amount is small, the paperwork can still matter. A correction keeps the account clean and
avoids a repeated excise tax. Keep the statements and the correction confirmation with your tax
records.

## What you can still pay from the HSA

After you stop contributing, you can generally still use the HSA to pay qualified medical expenses.
That includes many Medicare premiums, such as Part B, Part D, and Medicare Advantage premiums. It
does not include every insurance premium, and Medigap premiums are generally not qualified. Check
IRS Publication 969 for the current list.

The account can also pay deductibles, coinsurance, and other qualified medical costs, within the
rules. Keep receipts. A tax-free distribution has to be for a qualified expense, and the burden of
proof is on the account owner.

Do not use the HSA for a non-medical expense and assume it will be ignored. A non-qualified
distribution is generally taxable, and an additional tax can apply.

## Worked example

A person plans to enroll in Medicare and expects Part A to start on June 1. The six-month rule
reaches back to December 1 of the prior year. The person should stop HSA contributions before
December 1, not in May.

If the person contributed every month from January through May, those five months fall inside the
backdated coverage period. They can be treated as excess contributions even though the Medicare card
had not arrived. The fix is to remove the excess and the earnings, not to wait and hope the account
is overlooked.

If the person has premium-free Part A, the backdate does not create a Part A premium bill. It still
affects HSA eligibility. That is the part people miss: no premium does not mean no coverage.

## What counts as a qualifying plan

HSA contributions require a qualifying high-deductible health plan and no disqualifying coverage.
The plan has to meet the IRS minimum deductible and maximum out-of-pocket rules for the year. A plan
that looks like a high-deductible plan but fails those rules does not support a contribution.

Other coverage can disqualify you even if the plan itself qualifies. A general-purpose health
flexible spending account, a traditional Medicare enrollment, or coverage under another person’s
non-qualifying plan can end eligibility. A limited-purpose account tied to dental or vision costs
may be treated differently, but the rules are specific.

Do not assume that an employer calls a plan “HSA-eligible” and stop there. Ask for the plan
documents and check the IRS rules. If the plan changes mid-year, eligibility can change with it.

## A checklist for the stop date

Write down the expected Part A effective date. Count back six months. Tell the payroll or benefits
office the stop date. Confirm the final contribution and check the HSA custodian statement after the
change.

If you already contributed after the stop date, ask about a correction before the tax filing
deadline. Keep the correction confirmation with the return. If you are not sure whether a month
counts, ask the custodian and a tax preparer rather than guessing.

Review the HSA again when the Medicare card arrives. The card confirms the effective date, and the
date is what matters. A contribution made in good faith before the card arrived can still be excess
if the backdate reaches it.

## HSA contributions after Medicare enrollment

Once Medicare enrollment begins, new HSA contributions generally have to stop. The issue is not the
calendar date on the Medicare card. It is the date Part A coverage starts, which can be earlier than
the application date because of the six-month backdate.

A person who enrolls in Medicare at 65 should therefore stop contributions before the backdated
period begins. Waiting until the card arrives can leave contributions inside months that are now
covered by Part A. Those contributions can become excess contributions, and the tax rules for
correcting them depend on the facts.

Employer contributions count too. A payroll deduction that continues after the stop date can create
the same problem even if the employee did not write a check. Ask the employer to stop the
contribution and confirm the date in writing.

The HSA itself does not disappear. After enrollment, the account can still pay qualified medical
expenses, and the balance can stay invested. The restriction is on new contributions, not on using
the money that is already there. Keep receipts and make sure the expense is qualified.

If a contribution went in too late, do not ignore it. Ask the HSA custodian about a correction and
ask a tax preparer how to report it. The correction may involve a withdrawal of the excess and the
related earnings. The earlier the issue is found, the easier the fix.

The safest habit is to set a calendar reminder before the Medicare window and confirm the stop date with both the employer and the HSA custodian.

## Where the six-month rule gets complicated

1. The six-month stop date is a planning rule, not a substitute for the official effective date. A
   different Part A start date changes the calculation.
2. HSA correction rules have deadlines and reporting requirements. Ask the custodian and a tax
   preparer before removing or recharacterizing a contribution.
3. This page is guidance, not tax advice. Confirm the current HSA and Medicare rules with IRS
   Publication 969 and Medicare.gov.
