## What the Part A deductible is

The Part A deductible is the amount you pay for inpatient hospital care before Medicare begins to
pay its share. For {year}, it is {formatMoney(deductible)}. The important detail is the unit: it is
charged per benefit period, not per calendar year. A benefit period starts on the day you are
admitted as an inpatient and ends when you have been out of the hospital or skilled nursing facility
for 60 consecutive days. If a second admission happens more than 60 days after discharge, a new
benefit period begins and the deductible is owed again.

That structure means a person with two hospital stays in the same year, separated by more than 60
days, can owe the deductible twice. A person with a single long stay owes it once, plus the daily
coinsurance for the later days. The deductible is not an annual cap on exposure; it is a per-stay
threshold.

## Medicare Part A coinsurance {year}

## What Medicare pays after the deductible

After the deductible is met, Medicare pays the full approved amount for days 1 through 60 of the
hospital stay. You pay nothing more for the approved services during those days. For days 61
through 90, you pay {formatMoney(coinsurance61to90)} per day. After day 90, you can draw on up to 60
lifetime reserve days, each costing {formatMoney(lifetimeReserve)}. Those reserve days are not
renewed; once they are used, they are gone.

The daily amounts are per day, not per stay, so a 75-day stay costs 15 days at the{' '}
{formatMoney(coinsurance61to90)} rate. A stay that runs past 150 days total puts you past the
reserve days, and you pay all costs from that point. The daily coinsurance is not subject to any
annual out-of-pocket limit in Original Medicare, which is one reason supplemental coverage matters
for people with frequent or long hospitalizations.

## Skilled nursing and the 100-day rule

Part A also covers skilled nursing facility care after a qualifying hospital stay. Days 1 through 20
are covered at 0 dollars. Days 21 through 100 cost {formatMoney(snfCoinsurance)} per day. After day 100,
Medicare stops paying and you owe the full cost. The stay must be for skilled care, such as
rehabilitation or skilled nursing, not custodial care, and the benefit period rules apply here too.

The skilled nursing benefit is one of the most misunderstood parts of Medicare. A person who needs
help with daily activities but not skilled care does not qualify, and a person who qualifies can
lose the benefit if the care plan changes to custodial. The Part A deductible does not apply to
skilled nursing, but the daily coinsurance does.

## Who pays a Part A premium

Most people get Part A without a monthly premium because they or a spouse have at least 40 quarters
of Medicare-covered work. A person with 30 to 39 quarters pays {formatMoney(reducedPremium)} a
month for Part A. A person with fewer than 30 quarters pays {formatMoney(fullPremium)} a month.
To buy Part A, you must also enroll in Part B and pay both premiums.

The quarters are earned through work covered by Medicare taxes. A person can qualify on a spouse's
record, including a deceased or divorced spouse's record in some cases, and a person who worked in
a government job that paid Medicare taxes can qualify on that record. The work record should be
checked before budgeting, because the difference between 0 dollars and {formatMoney(fullPremium)} a month
is large over a year.

## What Part A does not cover

Part A does not cover most prescription drugs, physician services outside a hospital stay, routine
dental, vision, hearing, or long-term custodial care. A person who needs those services relies on
Part B, Part D, a Medicare Advantage plan, or out-of-pocket payment. Part A also does not cap
out-of-pocket spending for the year, so a long or repeated hospitalization can be expensive even
with Medicare.

The deductible and coinsurance are the same nationwide. They do not vary by state, by hospital, or
by the type of care. What varies is the amount of care a person uses and the supplemental coverage
chosen to fill the gaps.

## How to plan for the Part A costs

A person preparing for a possible hospitalization should plan for three separate exposures: the
deductible for each benefit period, the daily coinsurance for a long stay, and the Part A premium
if work quarters are short. The deductible is the most predictable, because it is a fixed amount
per benefit period. The daily coinsurance is the variable, because it depends on the length of the
stay and whether lifetime reserve days are used. The premium is the one that can be avoided by
checking the work record early, before the budget is set.

Supplemental coverage changes the picture. A Medigap policy can pay the Part A deductible and the
daily coinsurance, and a Medicare Advantage plan sets its own cost sharing with an annual
out-of-pocket limit. A person with either type of coverage should still understand the underlying
Medicare amounts, because the supplemental plan's cost sharing is usually built on top of them.

## Worked example

Consider a person admitted to the hospital for 8 days in March {year}. The person pays the{' '}
{formatMoney(deductible)} deductible. Because the stay is under 60 days, Medicare pays the rest of
the approved amount for the hospital care. The total out-of-pocket cost for the stay is{' '}
{formatMoney(deductible)}, plus any Part B charges for physician services billed separately.

Now suppose the same person is admitted again in August for 75 days, more than 60 days after the
March discharge. A new benefit period begins, so the person pays another {formatMoney(deductible)}.
Days 1 through 60 of the August stay are covered after that deductible. Days 61 through 75, which
is 15 days, cost {formatMoney(coinsurance61to90)} each, or {formatMoney(coinsurance61to90 * 15)}
for those 15 days. The total for the second stay is {formatMoney(deductible)} plus{' '}
{formatMoney(coinsurance61to90 * 15)}.

A third case: the person needs 30 days in a skilled nursing facility after the hospital stay. Days
1 through 20 cost nothing. Days 21 through 30, which is 10 days, cost {formatMoney(snfCoinsurance)}
each, or {formatMoney(snfCoinsurance * 10)}. That amount is separate from the hospital deductible
and is owed in addition to it.

## Where the Part A deductible can surprise you

1. The deductible is per benefit period, not per year, so two stays 61 days apart cost two
   deductibles.
2. Lifetime reserve days do not renew. A person who uses them has fewer left for a later year.
3. Skilled nursing coverage depends on the type of care. Custodial care is not covered even when
   it follows a hospital stay.
4. This page explains the standard figures. It does not estimate a specific person's hospital
   costs, because those depend on the care used and any supplemental coverage.

The figures are the standard Medicare amounts for {year}, checked against the CMS fact sheet on{' '}

the approved amount, so the setting and the provider matter. Before a planned stay, confirm whether

coinsurance.
