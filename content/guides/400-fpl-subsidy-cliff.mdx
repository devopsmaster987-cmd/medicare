## What the cliff is

Premium tax credits are calculated on a sliding scale below a certain income limit. The scale uses
the federal poverty guidelines and the applicable percentage table. Above the limit, the credit
generally stops rather than tapering to zero.

That stopping point is the cliff. A household below the line can receive help. A household above the
line can lose it, even if the income difference is small. The cliff is the reason a year-end bonus,
a capital gain, or a Roth conversion can change a Marketplace result.

The cliff is not the only thing that determines the credit. Below the line, the benchmark premium
and the applicable percentage still matter. A household can be under the cliff and still owe a
substantial monthly premium.

## ACA subsidy cliff extension {year}

The enhanced subsidy provisions are shown as expired in the current data layer. For {coverageYear},
the working assumption is that premium tax credits are generally limited to households at or below
{limitPercent} percent of the poverty line. A household above the line should not assume an
extension applies.

The law can change. A news article, a campaign proposal, or an old guide is not a substitute for the
current statute and the official Marketplace page. Check the status before you make an income
decision that depends on a subsidy.

If an extension is enacted, the cliff may move or disappear for a period. The data layer and this
guide would need a new official source and a new checked date before the page could say so. Until
then, the conservative reading is the one in the data.

## What counts toward the limit

The limit is based on modified adjusted gross income for the tax household. It is not based on take-
home pay, and it is not based on the income of the applicant alone. Add the income items the
Marketplace instructions include, and use the household members who belong on the tax return.

A one-time event can push a household over the line. A severance payment, a retirement distribution,
a business sale, or a Roth conversion can all raise MAGI. A tax-exempt interest payment can raise it
even though it is not taxable in the usual sense.

Do not assume that a deduction you claim on the return is ignored. The starting point is adjusted
gross income, so deductions that reduce AGI can help. Deductions that do not reduce AGI may not.
Use the official income page for the specific items.

## What to do near the line

If your estimate is near the cliff, build a cushion. A projection that lands exactly on the line can
cross it after a late dividend or a corrected form. A small cushion reduces the risk of losing the
credit because of an unexpected payment.

Ask whether a contribution to a health savings account, a retirement plan, or another tax-advantaged
account can lower MAGI. Ask whether a planned distribution can move to a different year. These are
tax decisions, so use a tax preparer.

Do not hide income or understate the projection to stay under the line. The credit is reconciled on
the tax return, and a wrong estimate can create a repayment. The goal is an accurate projection, not
a clever one.

A household just above the line, at {formatMoney(exampleLimit + 1)}, generally falls outside the
credit range under the current rules. The one-dollar difference can remove the credit, which is why
the cliff is a planning issue rather than a rounding issue.

The actual credit at the line still depends on the benchmark premium for the household. This page
does not invent a benchmark premium. It shows the income line, the contribution, and the rule that
changes at the line.

## Why the benchmark premium matters

The 400 percent line decides whether a household can receive a premium tax credit. It does not
decide the dollar amount. Below the line, the credit is the difference between the benchmark plan
premium and the household’s required contribution.

The benchmark is a specific plan used for the calculation, not the plan the household necessarily
buys. A household can choose a cheaper plan and pay less, or a more expensive plan and pay the
difference. The credit is tied to the benchmark, so the plan choice changes the out-of-pocket cost.

The cliff is also not a coupon. Being under the line does not mean the coverage is free, and being
over the line does not mean the household has no options. A household above the line can still buy a
plan at full price, use an employer offer, or qualify for Medicaid in some states. The credit is one
path, not the only path.

That is why this page does not show a single credit amount. The income limit and the contribution
are computable from the data layer. The benchmark premium is plan-specific and changes by area. Use
the official Marketplace tools for the dollar estimate.

## A checklist for a household near the line

Project the income before the year ends. Include every income source the Marketplace counts. Compare
the projection with the 400 percent limit for the household size. Write down the headroom.

If the headroom is small, review planned distributions, capital gains, and conversions. Ask whether
a contribution to a tax-advantaged account can lower MAGI. Ask whether a payment can move to another
year. Do not hide income or understate the projection.

Update the Marketplace if the income changes during the year. Keep the estimate, the documents, and
the application confirmation. At tax time, compare the credit received with the credit allowed. A
reconciliation is easier when the records are complete.

## Three honest caveats

1. The 400 percent line is a rule under the current data, not a permanent feature of the law. Check
   the current statute before you rely on it.
2. The cliff uses MAGI and the tax household, not gross pay and the applicant alone.
3. This page does not calculate a premium tax credit. It explains the income limit and the
   contribution at the top of the range.
