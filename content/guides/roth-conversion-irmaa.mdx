## Why a conversion can raise a Medicare premium

A Roth conversion moves money from a traditional retirement account into a Roth account. The
converted amount is generally included in income for the year of the conversion. That income raises
adjusted gross income and, in most cases, modified adjusted gross income as well.

IRMAA uses modified adjusted gross income from a prior year. The premium for {year} generally uses
income from {year - partB2026.lookbackYears}. A conversion in that lookback year can therefore raise
the Part B and Part D amounts for {year}. The Roth account may grow tax-free, but the Medicare
premium can arrive before the benefit feels real.

The conversion does not have to be enormous to matter. The question is proximity to a cliff. If the
conversion keeps income under the first threshold, the standard premium may still apply. If it
crosses the line by one dollar, the surcharge starts for the whole year.

## The two-year lookback in practice

The lookback creates a timing problem. In the year you convert, you may be focused on the income
tax. The Medicare premium shows up later, based on the same return. A conversion that looks
affordable in the tax software can still create an IRMAA surprise.

The practical fix is to plan the conversion with both years in view. Ask what the lookback year
income will be after the conversion, not just what the tax bill will be. Then compare that figure
with the IRMAA table for the premium year.

If you convert every year, the income may keep you in a higher tier every year. If you convert once,
the effect may last for the premium year that uses that return. The pattern matters as much as the
amount.

## Measure the headroom before you convert

Headroom is the distance from your income to the next bracket line. It is a more useful number than
the tier label because it tells you how much room a conversion has before the price changes.

Run the [IRMAA cliff checker](/calculators/irmaa-cliff-checker) with the projected income before the
conversion. Read the next tier start and subtract the projected income. That is the headroom. Then
decide how much to convert, or whether to split the conversion.

Do not treat headroom as a target. It is a boundary. Converting exactly up to the line leaves no
margin for a late dividend, a capital gain distribution, or a corrected tax form. A small cushion is
usually more comfortable than a perfectly optimized number.

## Mistakes that create a surprise

The most common mistake is using the income after the conversion for the tax estimate but forgetting
the two-year Medicare lag. The tax bill and the premium are related but not simultaneous.

Another mistake is converting in a year with an unusually high income, such as a year with a business
sale or a large capital gain. The combined income can cross several IRMAA tiers. The tax cost and
the Medicare cost both rise.

A third mistake is assuming that a later market loss undoes the conversion income. The conversion
income is reported in the year of the conversion. A market decline after the conversion does not
rewrite the return or the IRMAA decision.

## What to ask a tax preparer

Ask four questions before converting:

- What will modified adjusted gross income be after the conversion, including all income sources?
- Which IRMAA tier does that figure create in the premium year?
- How much headroom remains before the next tier?
- Would splitting the conversion across two years change the total tax and premium cost?

Bring the IRMAA table to the conversation. A tax preparer can model the tax, but the Medicare table
is a separate rule with its own cliff. The best decision usually considers both.

## Convert in a low-income year

The IRMAA effect is easier to manage when the conversion year is otherwise quiet. A year with no
salary, no business sale, and no large capital gain leaves more room before the first threshold. A
retiree who has not started Social Security may have a window with lower income.

That window is not automatic. A pension, an annuity, or a required distribution can fill it. A
conversion also raises the income used for other tests, including the Marketplace subsidy rules if
the household is not on Medicare yet. The conversion decision belongs in a whole-year plan.

If the window is short, a smaller conversion may be better than a large one that crosses a cliff.
The goal is not to convert everything at once. The goal is to use the room you have without paying
an avoidable premium penalty.

## The Part D side of the same decision

IRMAA has a Part B piece and a Part D piece. The Part B piece changes the medical premium. The Part D
piece is added to the drug plan premium, and it can apply even when the Part B amount is already
high.

A conversion that moves a household into a higher tier raises both pieces. The monthly difference in
the worked example includes both. If one spouse has no Part D plan, that spouse may not pay the Part D
amount, but the Part B amount can still apply. The household total depends on who is enrolled.

Do not compare only the Part B line. Read the full tier and the enrollment facts for each spouse.

## When a conversion is not worth the premium

A conversion is not worth it if the tax cost, the IRMAA cost, and the loss of other income-based
benefits together exceed the long-term benefit. That can happen when the conversion pushes income
over a subsidy cliff or a tax bracket at the same time as an IRMAA cliff.

It can also happen when the money will be spent soon. A Roth account is most valuable when it has
time to grow and when the owner expects a higher future tax rate. A conversion made only to fill a
bracket, without a plan for the money, can create costs without a clear benefit.

Run the numbers with a tax preparer. Use the IRMAA calculator for the premium side. Then decide
whether the combined result supports the conversion, a smaller conversion, or no conversion this
year.

## How much Roth conversion before IRMAA

The usable amount is the headroom to the next IRMAA threshold, not the size of the conversion you
would like to make. Start with projected MAGI before the conversion, find the threshold that applies
to the filing status, and subtract. The result is the room you have before the next tier.

That calculation answers a narrow question. It does not include the income tax on the conversion,
the effect on other credits, or the future tax rate. A conversion that fits under the IRMAA line can
still be a poor tax move, and a conversion that crosses the line can still be worthwhile if the tax
savings are larger than the Medicare surcharge.

The timing is two years removed. Income in the conversion year generally sets the premium two years
later. A conversion in the current year can therefore change a premium after the next tax return is
processed. Do not assume the premium will change immediately.

Splitting a conversion can preserve headroom. Converting part of the amount this year and part next
year may keep each year below a threshold, but it also spreads the tax and adds two years of
planning. The right answer depends on the brackets, the taxpayer's age, and the rest of the income
picture.

Use the IRMAA calculator to see the tier and the surcharge, then take the conversion amount to a tax
preparer. The calculator does not model the tax on the conversion. It shows the Medicare cost of
crossing a line, which is only one part of the decision.

## Where the Roth and IRMAA goals diverge

1. A Roth conversion can be a sound tax move and still raise IRMAA. The two results are not
   contradictory.
2. This page does not calculate the income tax on a conversion or promise a tax benefit. It only
   explains the Medicare premium interaction.
3. The IRMAA table changes by year. Confirm the current thresholds and the lookback year on the
   official sources before you convert.
