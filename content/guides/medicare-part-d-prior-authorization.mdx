## What prior authorization means

Prior authorization is a plan rule that requires approval before a prescription is covered. The plan wants to confirm that the drug is medically necessary and that the plan's rules are met. The review happens before the pharmacy fills the prescription.

The rule is not a denial. It is a checkpoint. Many requests are approved when the prescriber provides the right information. The problem is usually delay, not a permanent refusal.

A person who has been on a drug for years may still face prior authorization when switching plans. The new plan has its own rules. A drug that was covered without review under one plan may need review under another.

A person who is about to change plans should check the new plan's formulary and prior authorization rules before enrolling. That can prevent a gap at the pharmacy counter.

## Which drugs have the rule

Plans apply prior authorization to selected drugs. The list varies by plan. A plan may require review for expensive drugs, drugs with safety concerns, or drugs that have cheaper alternatives.

A plan may also use step therapy. That rule can require the person to try a preferred drug first. Prior authorization and step therapy are related but not the same. The plan's materials explain which rule applies.

A plan may use quantity limits. That rule caps how much of a drug the plan will cover in a period. A person who needs a higher amount may need an exception.

A person should check the plan's formulary and utilization management list each year. The rules can change, and a drug that was easy to fill last year may need review this year.

## How the request works

The prescriber usually submits the prior authorization request. The request includes the drug, the dose, the diagnosis, and the reason the drug is needed. The plan reviews the information and makes a decision.

The prescriber may need to show that other drugs were tried and did not work, or that they are not appropriate. The plan may ask for chart notes, lab results, or a letter of medical necessity.

A person can also call the plan to start the process. The plan can explain the form and the evidence. The person should write down the date, the representative's name, and the reference number.

A person who is waiting for a decision should ask the pharmacy whether it can hold the prescription. A short hold may be enough. A longer delay may require an urgent request.

## Urgent situations

A person who needs the drug quickly can ask for an expedited review. The plan decides whether the request qualifies. The prescriber should explain why waiting could harm the person.

An expedited review is not automatic. The plan applies its criteria. The person should ask the prescriber to document the urgency clearly.

A person who is discharged from a hospital or who is starting a new treatment may be in an urgent situation. The discharge planner or the prescriber's office can help with the request.

A person who cannot get the drug through the plan may have other options, such as a temporary supply or a different drug. The plan and the prescriber can explain the choices.

## If the plan says no

A denial notice should explain the reason and the appeal rights. The person should read the notice carefully and keep it. The notice is the roadmap for the next step.

The first appeal is usually a reconsideration by the plan. The person or the prescriber can provide more information. A letter from the prescriber can be important.

If the plan upholds the denial, the person may be able to request an independent review. The denial notice explains the levels and the deadlines. The person should follow the instructions.

A person who is not sure how to appeal can contact the plan, the state health insurance assistance program, or a Medicare rights organization. The person should keep the medical records and the denial notice.

## Planning ahead

A person who is choosing a Part D plan should check the formulary, the prior authorization list, and the pharmacy network. The lowest premium plan may not cover the person's drugs or may require more review.

A person who is keeping the same plan should check the annual notice of changes. The plan may add or remove drugs, change tiers, or add new rules. The notice usually arrives before the next plan year.

A person who is switching from one plan to another should ask the new plan whether the current drugs need review. A short call before the switch can prevent a delay.

A person who travels should check whether the plan's rules apply at out-of-area pharmacies. The plan's network and the prior authorization rules can affect a refill away from home.

## Worked example

Suppose a person takes a drug that the plan has placed under prior authorization. The person has been on the drug for a year, so the refill feels routine. The pharmacy says the plan needs approval.

The prescriber submits the request with the diagnosis, the dose, and the reason the drug is needed. The plan reviews the information and approves the request. The person fills the prescription a few days later.

A second person switches plans and does not check the new plan's rules. The first refill is delayed because the new plan requires prior authorization. The person asks the prescriber to submit the request and uses a temporary supply if available.

A third person is denied because the paperwork is incomplete. The person appeals with the missing records, and the plan approves the drug. The example shows why a complete request matters.

## Common mistakes

One mistake is waiting until the pharmacy rejects the claim. The request should start before the refill runs out. Another mistake is sending an incomplete request. The plan may deny it, and the person has to start over.

A third mistake is ignoring the plan's letter. The letter explains the reason and the appeal path. A person who reads it can respond with the right evidence.

A fourth mistake is assuming the rule applies only to new drugs. A plan can add the rule to a drug the person has used for years. The annual notice and the formulary are the place to check.

## What prior authorization does not settle

1. Prior authorization is a review, not an automatic denial. Many requests are approved with the right evidence.
2. The rules vary by plan and can change each year. Check the formulary and the annual notice.
3. A denial can be appealed, but the deadlines are short. Read the notice and act promptly.

## Ask the prescriber to start early

Check the plan's prior authorization list before a refill or a plan change. If a drug needs review, ask the prescriber's office to submit the request early. The
[Part D formulary exception guide](/guides/medicare-part-d-formulary-exception/) explains a related request, and the
[Part D late enrollment penalty guide](/guides/medicare-part-d-late-enrollment-penalty/) covers the drug penalty. If you are comparing plans, use the
[Medicare Savings Program calculator](/calculators/medicare-savings-program-extra-help/) to check for cost help.
