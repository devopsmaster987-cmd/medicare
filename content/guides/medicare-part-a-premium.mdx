## Who pays no Part A premium

Most people do not pay a monthly Part A premium. The standard rule is that a person needs 40 or more quarters of Medicare-covered work, or qualifying work from a spouse or former spouse. A quarter is a period of work covered by Social Security or the Railroad Retirement Board, and the amount of earnings needed for a quarter is set each year.

People who paid Medicare taxes for at least 40 quarters generally receive premium-free Part A. The work can be their own, a spouse's, or in some cases a deceased or former spouse's. The Social Security Administration makes the quarter determination when a person applies for Medicare.

Premium-free Part A does not mean Part A is free in every sense. A hospital stay can still involve the inpatient deductible and daily coinsurance for a long stay. The premium is only one part of the cost. Part B, Part D, and supplemental coverage have their own premiums and cost sharing.

A person who is not eligible for premium-free Part A can still enroll by paying a premium. The amount depends on the number of work quarters. This is the group that needs the most careful planning, because the premium can be a substantial monthly cost on top of Part B.

## The reduced premium for 30 to 39 quarters

A person with 30 to 39 quarters of Medicare-covered work may pay the reduced Part A premium. For{' '}
{year}, that amount is {formatMoney(reducedPremium)} a month. Across twelve months, the cost is{' '}
{formatMoney(reducedAnnual)}. This is not a small amount, and it is separate from the Part B premium.

The reduced premium is available to people who are not eligible for premium-free Part A but who have enough work history to qualify for the lower amount. The quarter count comes from the official record. A person who is close to 30 quarters should check the record before assuming the full premium applies.

A spouse's work history can matter. In some cases, a person can qualify for premium-free Part A through a spouse's work record even if their own record is short. A divorced spouse may also qualify under certain conditions. These rules are fact-specific, so the application and the Social Security record are the place to confirm.

The reduced premium is not an income-based amount. It is based on work history, not on current earnings. A high-income person with 32 quarters pays the reduced premium, and a low-income person with 24 quarters pays the full premium, before any other assistance is considered.

## The full premium for fewer than 30 quarters

A person with fewer than 30 quarters of Medicare-covered work may pay the full Part A premium. For{' '}
{year}, the full premium is {formatMoney(fullPremium)} a month, or {formatMoney(fullAnnual)} for a year. This is the highest Part A premium and applies only to people who are not eligible for the reduced amount.

The full premium can apply to recent immigrants, people who worked mainly outside the Medicare system, and some people with limited covered work history. It can also apply when a person does not have a qualifying spouse's record. The official determination is made when the person applies.

Some people in this group decide whether to enroll based on the cost of Part A and Part B together. A person who is not eligible for premium-free Part A may also be eligible for Marketplace coverage, and the decision can be complicated. The rule that Marketplace subsidies end when a person is eligible for premium-free Part A does not apply in the same way to someone who must pay for Part A.

The full premium does not include Part B. A person who enrolls in both parts pays both premiums. A person who delays Part B may face a late enrollment penalty later. The enrollment decision should account for all parts, not just the Part A premium.

## How work quarters are counted

A work quarter is based on earnings covered by Social Security. The earnings threshold for a quarter changes each year, and the maximum is four quarters per year. A person who worked for many years in covered employment usually has far more than 40 quarters.

Work under Social Security, the Railroad Retirement Board, and some government employment may count. Work that was not covered by Medicare taxes may not count, even if the person paid other taxes. The Social Security record is the official source, and a person can review it before applying.

A spouse's record can help. A person may qualify for premium-free Part A based on a spouse's work history, including a deceased spouse in some cases. A divorced spouse may qualify if the marriage lasted long enough and other conditions are met. These rules are applied by Social Security when the application is processed.

If the record is wrong or incomplete, a person can ask Social Security to correct it. Pay stubs, tax returns, and employment records can help. The correction should happen before enrollment if possible, because the premium depends on the quarter count.

## What Part A covers and what it does not

Part A helps pay for inpatient hospital care, skilled nursing facility care after a qualifying hospital stay, hospice care, and some home health care. It does not cover most outpatient care, doctor visits, or prescription drugs. Those are handled by Part B and Part D, or by a private plan.

The Part A inpatient deductible applies to each benefit period, not to a calendar year in the same way as the Part B deductible. A benefit period begins when a person enters a hospital and ends after a period without inpatient care. A long stay can involve daily coinsurance after the covered days.

Part A does not cover long-term custodial care in a nursing home. It covers skilled care for a limited time when the coverage rules are met. A person who needs help with daily activities, rather than skilled care, may need to pay privately or use another program.

The Part A premium is only one part of the cost of hospital coverage. The deductible, the coinsurance, and any supplemental policy matter too. A person who is deciding whether to pay for Part A should look at the full cost of coverage, not just the monthly premium.

## What the Part A premium leaves out

1. The quarter count is based on the official work record, not on a person's own estimate. Check the record with Social Security before assuming a premium amount.
2. The Part A premium is separate from Part B, Part D, and supplemental coverage. A full budget includes all of them.
3. A person who must pay for Part A may have different Marketplace options than a person who is eligible for premium-free Part A. The decision should be checked against the current rules.

## Check the work record before budgeting

If you are not sure how many work quarters you have, review your Social Security record before you apply. If you must pay a Part A premium, compare the total cost of Part A and Part B with the alternatives available to you. The
[Medicare vs ACA decision guide](/calculators/medicare-vs-aca) covers the case of people who do not qualify for premium-free Part A. The
[Medicare Savings Program and Extra Help calculator](/calculators/medicare-savings-program-extra-help)
can help you check whether you may qualify for help with premiums.
