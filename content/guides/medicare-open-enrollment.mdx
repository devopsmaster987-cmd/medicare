## Medicare open enrollment dates {year}

The annual enrollment period is the yearly window when a person with Medicare can change coverage.
It runs from October 15 through December 7, and any change made during it takes effect January 1.
During this window, a person can switch from one Medicare Advantage plan to another, switch from
Medicare Advantage to Original Medicare, switch from Original Medicare to Medicare Advantage, or
change Part D plans. A person can also drop drug coverage, although that can create a penalty if
the person later wants coverage again.

The annual window is the only time most people can make these changes without a qualifying event.
A person who is happy with the current plan does not need to do anything; coverage continues. A
person who wants to change should compare plans during the window, because premiums, formularies,
and networks change each year.

## What you can change during the window

Four categories of change are allowed. First, Medicare Advantage plan changes: switching to a
different Medicare Advantage plan, or switching between Medicare Advantage and Original Medicare.
Second, Part D plan changes: switching to a different Part D plan, or adding Part D if you are
in Original Medicare without it. Third, Medicare Advantage with drug coverage changes: switching
between integrated plans. Fourth, dropping coverage: leaving Medicare Advantage for Original
Medicare, or dropping Part D, which may trigger a penalty if you later re-enroll.

The window does not change Part A or Part B enrollment. Those are handled through Social Security
and are governed by the initial, general, and special enrollment periods. A person who wants to
add Part B outside the initial window needs a special enrollment period or the general enrollment
period.

## Medicare open enrollment vs general enrollment

The two windows are often confused. The annual enrollment period is for people who already have
Medicare and want to change plans. The general enrollment period, from January 1 through March
31, is for people who did not enroll in Part A or Part B when first eligible and do not have a
special enrollment period. Coverage from the general enrollment period starts the month after
enrollment, and a late enrollment penalty may apply.

A person who is in the general enrollment period because of a missed window should also check
whether a special enrollment period applies. The special enrollment period is usually better,
because it avoids the penalty and starts coverage faster. The annual enrollment period is not a
substitute for either one.

## Medicare special enrollment periods

A special enrollment period lets a person enroll in or change Medicare coverage outside the annual
window after a qualifying life event. Common events include losing employer or union coverage,
moving out of a plan's service area, moving into or out of a nursing home, and certain other
changes in circumstances. The period usually lasts a limited number of months after the event,
and the exact length depends on the event.

Loss of employer coverage is the most common trigger. A person who loses coverage based on current
employment has an eight-month window to enroll in Part B without a penalty, and a shorter window
to enroll in Part D or a Medicare Advantage plan. The person should enroll the month before the
coverage ends, so the start dates line up without a gap.

## Medigap open enrollment

The Medigap open enrollment period is a one-time six-month window that starts when Part B begins.
During it, an insurer generally cannot refuse to sell a Medigap policy or charge more because of
health status. After the window closes, medical underwriting can apply, and a policy can be
harder or more expensive to buy. This is separate from the annual enrollment period, which does
not reopen Medigap underwriting protections.

A person who is turning 65 and choosing between Original Medicare with Medigap and a Medicare
Advantage plan should make that choice with the Medigap window in mind. Delaying can close the
window and limit options later.

## How to use the annual window well

Five habits make the annual window productive. First, review the annual notice of change that the
plan sends in September, which lists the premium, deductible, and formulary changes for the coming
year. Second, compare the total annual cost on your own prescription list, not just the premium.
Third, check whether your doctors and pharmacy are still in the network. Fourth, confirm whether
a drug you take has moved to a higher tier or off the formulary. Fifth, enroll before the
December 7 deadline, because the system gets busy in the final days.

A person who does nothing keeps the current plan. That is a valid choice when the plan still fits.
The risk is not doing nothing; the risk is not checking, because a plan that fit last year may
not fit this year.

## Worked example

Consider a person in Original Medicare with a Part D plan. In October, the person receives the
annual notice of change and sees that the Part D premium rose and a drug moved to a higher tier.
The person uses the plan finder to compare three other Part D plans on the same prescription list
and finds one with a lower total annual cost. The person switches during the annual window, and
the new plan starts January 1. No penalty applies because the person maintained creditable
coverage.

A second person is in a Medicare Advantage plan and learns that the primary care doctor is leaving
the network. During the annual window, the person switches to a different Medicare Advantage plan
that includes the doctor. The new plan starts January 1. If the person instead switched to
Original Medicare, the person would need to buy a Part D plan during the same window to avoid a
gap in drug coverage.

A third person misses the December 7 deadline. The person stays in the current plan for another
year and must wait for the next annual window or a qualifying event. The missed deadline is not
fixable later, which is why the September notice and the October start date matter.

## What the annual window does not cover

1. The annual window changes plans, not Part A or Part B enrollment. Those are governed by
   different periods.
2. The window does not reopen Medigap underwriting protections. That is a separate one-time
   window tied to Part B.
3. A plan change during the window takes effect January 1. It does not take effect immediately.
4. This page explains the windows and deadlines. It does not recommend a specific plan, because
   the right plan depends on doctors, drugs, and budget.
