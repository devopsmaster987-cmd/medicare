## What the family glitch was

Before the fix, the affordability test for a whole family often used only the employee-only premium.
If that single premium was affordable, the family could be treated as having affordable coverage even
when family coverage cost far more. The result was a gap: the employee was offered coverage, but the
family could not afford to add anyone.

The fix changed the test for family members. The family premium now matters when the Marketplace
decides whether the family members have an affordable offer. The employee’s own test still uses the
employee-only premium. That split is the heart of the rule.

The change did not make every family eligible for help. The family still has to meet the income
rules, the minimum value rules, and the other Marketplace requirements. It also has to deal with the
400 percent limit under the current law.

## The two tests

The first test is for the employee. Compare the annual employee-only premium with the affordability
percentage of household income. If the premium is at or below the threshold, the employee-only offer
is affordable for the employee.

The second test is for the family members. Compare the annual family premium with the same
affordability percentage. If the family premium is above the threshold, the family members may be
able to get Marketplace help. The employee can still be ineligible because the employee-only offer
is affordable.

The two results can point in different directions. That is not a contradiction. It is the design of
the fix. A family can have an affordable offer for the worker and an unaffordable offer for the
spouse and children.

## What counts as minimum value

Affordability is only one part of the employer coverage test. The plan also has to provide minimum
value. A plan that pays a very small share of covered costs may fail the minimum value test even if
the premium is low.

If the plan fails minimum value, the employee and the family may have a path to Marketplace help.
The exact test is technical, and the employer plan documents are the place to start. Ask the
employer for the summary of benefits and coverage and the minimum value statement.

Do not assume that a low premium means good coverage. A cheap plan with a narrow network or a high
cost-sharing structure can still fail the test. The Marketplace asks about the offer, so the answer
needs to match the documents.

## A worked example

A household earns {formatMoney(income)}. The affordability threshold is{' '}
{formatPercent(rate)} of income, or {formatMoney(threshold)} a year. The employee-only premium is{' '}
{formatMoney(employeePremium)} a month, or {formatMoney(annualEmployee)} a year. That is below the
threshold, so the employee-only offer is affordable for the employee.

The family premium is {formatMoney(familyPremium)} a month, or {formatMoney(annualFamily)} a year.
That is above the {formatMoney(threshold)} threshold, so the family coverage is unaffordable for the
family members under the test. The family members may qualify for Marketplace help, while the
employee generally does not.

The result changes if the family premium falls. If the family premium were below the threshold, the
family members would be treated as having an affordable offer. The result also changes if the
household income is different, because the threshold moves with income.

## What to tell the Marketplace

Tell the Marketplace about the employer offer, the premium for employee-only coverage, and the
premium for family coverage. Answer the questions about minimum value and the plan’s coverage. Keep
the employer documents.

Do not say the coverage is unaffordable just because the family premium feels high. The test uses the
percentage and the actual premium. Do not say the employee is uninsured if the employee declined an
affordable offer. The facts need to match the records.

If the application asks for the lowest-cost plan, use the lowest-cost option that meets the rules,
not the plan the family would prefer. The affordability test is about the offer, not the choice.

## How the Marketplace verifies the offer

The application asks whether an employer offered coverage, what the coverage cost, and whether it met
minimum value. The Marketplace may check the answers against employer reporting and other data. A
mismatch can trigger a request for documents.

Keep the employer offer letter, the summary of benefits and coverage, and the premium sheet. If the
offer changes during the year, update the application. A change in the family premium can change the
affordability result.

Do not describe the offer from memory. Use the documents. The difference between the employee-only
premium and the family premium is the whole point of the fix, so both numbers need to be right.

## What happens at tax time

Premium tax credits are reconciled on the tax return. If the household received more credit than it
was allowed, part or all of the excess may have to be repaid. If it received less, the difference may
be added to the refund.

The reconciliation uses the income and household facts on the return. A mid-year change in income,
marital status, or dependents can change the result. Report changes to the Marketplace when they
happen, and keep the year-end tax forms.

The family glitch fix changes the eligibility test, not the reconciliation. A family that qualifies
because the family premium is unaffordable still has to report income accurately and stay within the
other rules.

## A checklist for the application

Confirm the tax household and who claims each person. Confirm the employer offer and both premiums.
Confirm whether the plan meets minimum value. Project the household income using the MAGI rules.
Check the 400 percent limit before assuming a credit.

Gather the documents and keep a copy of the application. If the Marketplace asks for proof, respond
by the deadline. If the offer changes, update the application rather than waiting for the tax return.

Write a short note for each answer. For example: “Employee-only premium is this amount per month;
family premium is that amount; the plan meets minimum value; the household income projection is this
figure.” A note like that makes the application easier to review and easier to correct if a number
changes. It also helps a tax preparer see why the family members were treated as eligible while the
employee was not, and it keeps the reasoning available if the Marketplace asks a follow-up question
later in the year.

## Three honest caveats

1. The family glitch fix does not create a subsidy for everyone. The household still has to meet the
   income rules and the other eligibility tests.
2. The employee and the family can have different results. One affordable offer does not decide the
   whole household.
3. The enhanced subsidy rules can change. Check the current law and the official Marketplace page
   before relying on the 400 percent limit.
