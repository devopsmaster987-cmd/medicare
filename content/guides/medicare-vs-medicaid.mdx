## What Medicare is

Medicare is federal health insurance. Most people become eligible at 65, and younger people can
qualify through disability, ALS, or end-stage renal disease. The program has four parts. Part A
covers hospital care, Part B covers physician and outpatient care, Part C is Medicare Advantage,
and Part D covers prescription drugs. The rules, premiums, and deductibles are the same nationwide,
and the program is run by the federal government.

Medicare is not based on income. A person with a high income pays the same standard Part B premium
as a person with a low income, although a higher income can trigger an income-related surcharge.
A person with a low income does not pay less for Medicare itself, but can qualify for state
programs that help with the cost.

## What Medicaid is

Medicaid is a joint federal and state program that helps pay health care costs for people with
limited income and, in some cases, limited assets. The federal government sets minimum standards,
and each state runs its own program with its own eligibility rules, covered benefits, and provider
networks. That means a person who qualifies in one state might not qualify in another, and the
benefits can differ.

Medicaid covers services that Medicare does not, including long-term nursing home care, personal
care, and some dental, vision, and hearing services. Medicaid cost sharing is low, and many
beneficiaries pay nothing for covered services. Medicaid also pays Medicare premiums and other
out-of-pocket costs for people who qualify for a Medicare Savings Program.

## Medicare vs Medicaid at a glance

The two programs differ in four ways that matter most. First, the basis of eligibility: age or
disability for Medicare, income for Medicaid. Second, who runs it: the federal government for
Medicare, the state for Medicaid. Third, the cost to the beneficiary: Medicare has premiums,
deductibles, and coinsurance with no annual cap in Original Medicare, while Medicaid cost sharing
is low or zero. Fourth, the scope of benefits: Medicare is acute and post-acute medical care,
while Medicaid adds long-term care and services Medicare excludes.

A person can qualify for both. People who have both are called dual eligible. Medicare pays first
for any service it covers, and Medicaid pays after Medicare and any other insurance. Medicaid can
pay the Part B premium, the deductible, and the coinsurance, and can cover drugs that Medicare does
not. A person who is dual eligible usually has very low out-of-pocket costs.

## Can you have both Medicare and Medicaid

Yes. A person who qualifies for Medicare by age or disability and also meets the income and
asset rules in their state can have both. Medicare is the primary payer and Medicaid is secondary.
Medicaid can also pay the Part B premium through a Medicare Savings Program, which makes Medicare
affordable even when the premium would otherwise be a burden.

People who are dual eligible are often enrolled in a special plan that coordinates the two
programs. These plans vary by state and by county, and the details should be checked with the
state Medicaid office. The Medicare Savings Programs are the main route for people who are not
fully dual eligible but still need help with Medicare costs.

## Medicare Savings Programs

Medicare Savings Programs are state programs that help pay Medicare costs for people with limited
income. There are four tiers. The Qualified Medicare Beneficiary program pays the Part A and Part
B premiums, deductibles, and coinsurance. The Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary program
pays the Part B premium. The Qualifying Individual program also pays the Part B premium. The
Qualified Disabled Working Individual program pays the Part A premium for certain disabled workers.

The income limits vary by state, and some states set them higher than the federal floor. A person
who qualifies for any Medicare Savings Program also qualifies automatically for Extra Help with
Part D costs. Applications go through the state Medicaid office, and the state can confirm which
tier applies.

## Who should apply for which program

A person turning 65 should apply for Medicare regardless of income, because the enrollment window
is time-limited and late enrollment carries a permanent penalty. The same person should apply for
Medicaid or a Medicare Savings Program if income is limited, because the programs can remove or
reduce the Medicare costs. Applying for both at the same time is common and is not a problem.

A person under 65 with a disability should apply for Medicare after the required waiting period,
and should apply for Medicaid in the meantime if income is limited. Medicaid can cover care during
the Medicare waiting period, and can continue to help after Medicare starts.

## Worked example

Consider a person who turns 65 with a monthly income below the state Medicaid limit. The person
enrolls in Medicare during the initial enrollment period, which gives Part A and Part B. The
person also applies for Medicaid and qualifies for the Qualified Medicare Beneficiary program. As
a result, Medicaid pays the Part B premium, the Part A and Part B deductibles, and the
coinsurance. The person still needs drug coverage, so the person enrolls in Part D and qualifies
for Extra Help, which pays the premium and most of the drug cost sharing.

A second person has the same Medicare enrollment but an income slightly above the Medicaid limit.
The person does not qualify for full Medicaid, but does qualify for the Specified Low-Income
Medicare Beneficiary program, which pays the Part B premium. The person still pays the Part A and
Part B deductibles and coinsurance, and must buy Part D or a Medicare Advantage plan. The
difference between the two cases is the income relative to the state limit.

A third person has an income well above the limits. The person pays the standard Medicare costs
and has no Medicaid. The person still benefits from knowing the program exists, because a drop
in income later, such as retirement or the death of a spouse, can change eligibility at any time.

## Where the rules can surprise you

1. Medicaid rules vary by state, so a figure or benefit that applies in one state may not apply
   in another. The state Medicaid office is the reliable source.
2. Medicare Savings Program income limits are not the same as Medicaid eligibility limits. A
   person can qualify for one and not the other.
3. Having both programs does not mean Medicare is free in every sense. The programs pay specific
   costs, and some services may still have a small charge.
4. This page is general information. It does not determine eligibility for any person, because
   that depends on state rules and individual circumstances.

## Where to check your own eligibility

Medicare eligibility is determined by Social Security, and the application is made through Social
Security. Medicaid and Medicare Savings Program eligibility is determined by the state, and the
application goes through the state Medicaid office. A person who is unsure should contact the state
SHIP program for free counseling, and can also use the official Medicare and Medicaid websites.
