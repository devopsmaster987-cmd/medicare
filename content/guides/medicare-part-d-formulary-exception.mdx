## What a formulary is

A formulary is a plan's list of covered prescription drugs. Each Part D plan has its own formulary, and the list can change each year. A drug that is covered by one plan may not be covered by another.

Formularies usually group drugs into tiers. A lower tier generally costs less, and a higher tier generally costs more. The tier placement is part of the plan's design, not a statement about whether the drug works.

A plan may also use utilization management rules. Prior authorization, step therapy, and quantity limits are common. These rules can require the prescriber to provide information before the plan pays.

A person who needs a drug that is not on the formulary, or who needs a lower tier, can ask for an exception. The request is a formal process, not a casual phone call.

## The types of exceptions

A formulary exception can ask the plan to cover a drug that is not on the formulary. The plan may approve the request if the drug is medically necessary and the covered alternatives are not appropriate.

A tiering exception can ask the plan to charge a lower cost-sharing tier for a covered drug. The plan may approve the request if the drug is medically necessary and the lower tier is appropriate.

A person may also need an exception to a utilization management rule. That is often handled as a coverage determination rather than a formulary exception. The plan's materials explain the correct process.

A person who is not sure which request applies should call the plan and ask. The plan can explain the form, the evidence, and the timeline. The person should write down the date and the representative's name.

## Who can request an exception

A person enrolled in the plan can request an exception. A prescriber can also request one on the person's behalf. In practice, the prescriber's office usually submits the request because the clinical information comes from the prescriber.

A person who is new to the plan should check the formulary before the first fill. A drug that was covered last year may be on a different tier or may be removed. The plan's annual notice explains the changes.

A person who is switching plans should compare formularies before enrolling. A plan with a low premium may not cover the drugs the person needs. The formulary and the pharmacy network matter as much as the premium.

A person who is in a nursing home or has a complex condition may need extra help. The plan's case management or the state's Senior Medicare Patrol may be able to explain the options.

## The prescriber's role

The prescriber's support is usually the key to an exception. The prescriber may need to explain why the formulary drug is not appropriate, what the person has already tried, and what harm could result from switching.

The plan may ask for chart notes, lab results, or a statement of medical necessity. The prescriber's office should send the information promptly. A request without clinical support is more likely to be denied.

A person should ask the prescriber's office whether it has handled formulary exceptions before. A practice that knows the process can avoid delays. The person should give the office the plan name and the member ID.

A person who cannot reach the prescriber should call the plan for help. The plan can explain what is missing. The person should keep a record of the calls. Ask the plan for the specific form and the documents it wants. A complete request is usually faster than a corrected one. Keep a copy of everything you send.

## How the request works

The person or the prescriber submits the request to the plan. The plan reviews the information and makes a decision. The plan must follow the coverage determination rules, including the timeframes for standard and expedited requests.

A request may be expedited if the person's health could be seriously harmed by waiting. The plan decides whether the request qualifies for an expedited review. The prescriber should state why speed matters.

If the plan approves the request, the person can fill the prescription under the approved terms. The approval may be for a limited time. The person should check the dates and the quantity.

If the plan denies the request, the denial notice should explain the reason and the appeal rights. The person should read the notice carefully and keep it.

## If the plan says no

A denial is not always the end. The person can appeal the decision. The first appeal is usually a reconsideration by the plan. The person or the prescriber can provide more information.

If the plan upholds the denial, the person may be able to request an independent review by an outside reviewer. The denial notice explains the levels and the deadlines. The person should follow the instructions in the notice.

A person who needs the drug urgently should ask for an expedited appeal. The prescriber can support the request. The plan must follow the expedited rules if the request qualifies.

A person who is not sure how to appeal can contact the plan, the state health insurance assistance program, or the Medicare rights organization in their state. The person should keep the denial notice and the medical records.

## Worked example

Suppose a person with a chronic condition takes a drug that is not on the plan's formulary. The plan covers a similar drug, but the person has already tried it and had side effects.

The prescriber submits a formulary exception request. The request explains the prior trial, the side effects, and why the non-formulary drug is medically necessary. The plan reviews the information and approves the request for a limited period.

A second person takes a drug that is on a high tier. The prescriber requests a tiering exception and explains why the lower tier is appropriate. The plan approves the lower cost-sharing tier.

A third person's request is denied because the paperwork is incomplete. The person appeals with the missing records, and the plan reverses the decision. The example shows why the evidence and the deadlines matter.

## Three honest caveats

1. An exception is not guaranteed. The plan reviews the medical necessity and the plan rules.
2. The approval may be time-limited. Check the dates and renew if needed.
3. A denial can be appealed, but the deadlines are short. Read the notice and act promptly.

## What to do next

Check the plan's formulary before you fill a new prescription. If the drug is not covered or the tier is too high, ask the prescriber's office to start an exception request. Keep the denial notice if the plan says no. The
[Part D late enrollment penalty guide](/guides/medicare-part-d-late-enrollment-penalty) explains the drug penalty, and the
[Part D out of pocket cap guide](/guides/part-d-out-of-pocket-cap) explains the annual cost limit. If you are comparing plans, use the
[Medicare Savings Program calculator](/calculators/medicare-savings-program-extra-help) to check for cost help.
