## What the cap covers

Part D has an annual limit on what you pay out of pocket for covered drugs. The limit is not a
limit on the premium. It is a limit on the cost-sharing that counts toward the plan’s out-of-pocket
threshold.

After you reach the cap, the plan generally pays for covered drugs for the rest of the year. You may
still pay a premium, and you may still pay for drugs the plan does not cover. The cap is a protection
for covered spending, not a promise that the whole year is free.

The cap does not apply to every cost. It does not cover the monthly premium. It does not cover a
drug that is excluded from the plan formulary. It does not cover a pharmacy that is out of network
unless the plan allows an exception. Read the plan’s evidence of coverage for the specific rules.

## Part D out of pocket cap {year} amount

The cap for {year} is {formatMoney(cap)}. That is the annual covered-drug limit reflected in the data
layer. The standard Part D deductible for {year} is {formatMoney(deductible)}, and the base
beneficiary premium is {formatMoney(basePremium)} a month.

The base premium is a national reference, not the price of every plan. A plan can charge more or
less. A plan with a higher premium can still be the better choice if its formulary covers the drugs
you take. Compare total cost, not just the premium.

The cap is not reduced by the deductible in a way that makes the first {formatMoney(deductible)}
free. The deductible is part of the covered spending that can count toward the cap, but you still
pay it at the pharmacy under the plan rules.

## What does not count

Premiums do not count toward the cap. The monthly plan premium and any Part D IRMAA amount are
separate. Paying a high premium does not move you closer to the cap.

Drugs that are not covered by the plan generally do not count. A drug that requires prior
authorization and is not approved may not count. A drug purchased outside the plan’s network may not
count. The plan’s formulary and network rules decide.

Costs that are not for covered Part D drugs generally do not count. That includes most over-the-
counter products, unless the plan covers them as a covered benefit. It includes services covered by
Part B, such as doctor visits, even when they happen in the same year.

## How the cap interacts with Extra Help

Extra Help is the Part D low-income subsidy. It can lower the premium, the deductible, and the copays
for covered drugs. It does not change the out-of-pocket cap into a different number, but it can make
the costs before the cap much lower.

If you have Extra Help, check the plan’s low-income cost-sharing rules and the Extra Help amount for
the year. The [Medicare Savings Program and Extra Help](/calculators/medicare-savings-program-extra-help/)
calculator can show whether the income and resource tests are likely met.

If you do not have Extra Help, the cap still applies, but you may reach it only after paying the
deductible and the plan’s copays. A plan with a lower premium but a restrictive formulary can cost
more overall.

## Worked example

A person pays the base beneficiary premium of {formatMoney(basePremium)} a month for a full year.
The premium total is {formatMoney(annualBasePremium)}. The person also reaches the {formatMoney(cap)}
out-of-pocket cap for covered drugs.

The rough total for the year is {formatMoney(premiumPlusCap)}, before any IRMAA amount and before
plan-specific premium differences. That is not a quote. It is an illustration of why the premium and
the cap are separate numbers.

If the same person has a plan premium of a different amount, the premium part changes while the cap
stays the same. If the person has IRMAA, the IRMAA add-on also stays outside the cap. That is why a
total-cost estimate needs the plan premium, the drugs, and the income-based add-on.

## How to compare two Part D plans

Start with the drugs you take and the pharmacy you use. Check the formulary, the tier, and the
network. A plan with a low premium can cost more if a regular drug is on a high tier or requires
prior authorization.

Add the annual premium, the deductible, and the expected copays for the year. Then compare the total
with the cap. A plan that reaches the cap quickly may be better for someone with expensive drugs. A
plan with a low premium may be better for someone with few prescriptions.

Do not compare only the premium or only the cap. The total depends on the drugs, the plan rules, and
the pharmacy. Use the plan’s own pricing tools and the Medicare Plan Finder for the estimate.

## What to do if a drug is not covered

Ask the plan for a formulary exception or a prior authorization. The plan has a process, and the
doctor usually supplies the clinical reason. A drug that is not covered may still be approved by
exception, but the process takes time.

If the drug is not covered and no exception is granted, the cost may not count toward the cap. That
can make the annual total much higher than the cap suggests. Ask the plan in writing how the drug
will be treated.

A temporary transition fill may be available when a plan changes its formulary. Ask the plan and the
pharmacist about the transition rules. Do not assume the old price will continue.

## A checklist for the annual review

List the drugs, doses, and pharmacies. Check the plan’s formulary and network for the coming year.
Compare the premium, deductible, and expected copays. Confirm whether the plan’s out-of-pocket cap is
the standard one and whether any drugs are excluded.

Check the Part D IRMAA amount separately. It is not part of the plan premium or the cap. If income
changed, check the tier before the annual enrollment period.

Keep the plan documents and the pharmacy receipts. If a claim is processed incorrectly, the receipts
and the explanation of benefits are the evidence. Ask the plan to correct the claim promptly.

Review the plan every year, even if the drugs have not changed. Formularies, tiers, and premiums can
change, and a plan that was the best fit last year may not be the best fit now.

## Does Part D out of pocket cap include premiums

No. The Part D out-of-pocket cap applies to covered drug costs, not to the monthly premium. The
premium is a separate charge, and any IRMAA amount is separate again. A person can reach the cap and
still owe the premium every month.

The cap also does not count every dollar spent at a pharmacy. It counts the covered drug cost
sharing that the plan rules include. A drug that is not covered, a purchase from an out-of-network
pharmacy, or a service that is not a Part D benefit may not count toward the cap. The plan's
evidence of coverage and the Medicare drug cost page are the places to check.

Extra Help can change the picture. A person who qualifies for Extra Help may pay lower premiums and
cost sharing, and the cap may be reached differently. The cap is a protection, not a promise that
the total cost of care is limited to one number.

When comparing plans, compare more than the premium. Add the premium, the deductible, the copays
for the drugs the person actually takes, and the network. A low-premium plan with a narrow network
can cost more overall than a higher-premium plan that covers the drugs.

If a drug is not covered, ask about an exception or a formulary alternative before paying cash. The
plan's exception process and the prescriber's documentation can change the cost. Keep the receipts
and the explanation of benefits so the cap calculation can be checked.

The cap is annual, so the calendar resets each plan year. A drug purchased in December does not carry its cost sharing into January.

## What the Part D cap leaves outside

1. The cap is for covered drugs. A plan can still charge for drugs it does not cover, and a
   formulary exception is not guaranteed.
2. The premium and IRMAA are outside the cap. A low drug bill does not mean a low total Medicare
   cost.
3. Part D plan premiums and formularies vary. Compare the plan documents for the drugs you take
   before choosing a plan.
