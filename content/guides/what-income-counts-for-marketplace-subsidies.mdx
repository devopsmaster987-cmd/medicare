## The income figure is MAGI

The Marketplace does not ask for gross pay and stop there. It starts with adjusted gross income and
adds several items that can be easy to miss. The result is modified adjusted gross income, usually
called MAGI.

The practical rule is to use the tax return you expect to file, then add the income items the
Marketplace instructions list. Tax-exempt interest, foreign earned income that is excluded from
taxable income, and certain other items can raise MAGI even when they do not feel like take-home
pay. If you are unsure, use the official income page and ask a tax preparer.

Do not use last year's tax return without checking whether this year looks different. A new job, a
raise, a retirement distribution, or a business profit can change the estimate. The application is
built around a projection, not a history lesson.

## Whose income counts

Household income is not only the applicant's income. It includes the income of everyone in the tax
household who is required to file a return, plus the income of people who are claimed as
dependents in some situations. Marriage, divorce, a new dependent, or a child leaving the household
can all change the figure.

That is why two people with the same salary can qualify for different amounts. The household
composition changes the poverty guideline and the income that is counted. A single applicant with a
child is not compared with a single applicant who lives alone.

If a household member files separately, the rules can be more complicated. Do not assume that one
person's return is the whole story. Use the official household income rules and, when the facts are
unusual, ask the Marketplace or a tax preparer.

## Which poverty guideline year applies

For {coverageYear} coverage, the Marketplace uses the poverty guidelines published for {fplYear}.
This is a common source of confusion. The table is one year behind the coverage year because the
Marketplace uses the prior-year guidelines for the premium tax credit calculation.

For a household of {householdSize}, the {fplYear} annual poverty guideline is {formatMoney(fpl)}.
That figure is the base for the percentage tests. The 400 percent limit for the same household is
{formatMoney(fourHundred)}.

Alaska and Hawaii use higher guidelines. A household of more than eight people adds an amount for
each additional person. Use the official table for the exact figure.

## The 400 percent limit

Under the rules reflected in the current data, premium tax credits generally end above 400 percent
of the federal poverty line. That is a cliff, not a gradual reduction. A household just above the
line can lose the credit, while a household just below it can still receive help.

The enhanced subsidy provisions are shown as expired in the data layer. That means a household
above the 400 percent line should not assume an extension exists. The law can change, so check the
current status before you rely on a guide or an old news article.

The 400 percent rule is not the only limit. Even below the line, the amount of help depends on the
benchmark plan premium, the household income, and the applicable percentage. A household can be
below the line and still pay a substantial share of the premium.

## What changes MAGI

Income from a job, self-employment, unemployment, Social Security, pensions, and investments can all
count. A Roth conversion can count. Tax-exempt interest can count. A one-time capital gain can
count. The list is broad, and the details depend on the tax return.

Some items are excluded or treated differently. A qualified distribution from a Roth account is
generally not taxable income, but the rest of the return still matters. A loan is not income. A
reimbursement is not income. A gift is not income. The labels people use in conversation are not
the same as the lines on a tax return.

The safest approach is to build the estimate from the return, line by line, then compare it with
the Marketplace instructions. If the number is close to a cliff, a small error can change the
result.

## Worked example

A household of {householdSize} expects {formatMoney(exampleIncome)} of MAGI for the coverage year.
The prior-year poverty guideline is {formatMoney(fpl)}. The household income is about{' '}
{formatNumber(examplePercent)} percent of the poverty line, which is below the 400 percent limit of{' '}
{formatMoney(fourHundred)}.

At that income, the applicable percentage is {formatPercent(aca2026.affordabilityPercentage.rate)}.
The expected annual contribution toward a benchmark plan is {formatMoney(requiredContribution)}, or
about {formatMoney(monthlyContribution)} a month. The actual premium tax credit depends on the
benchmark premium for the household and the plan chosen. This page does not invent a benchmark
premium, so it stops at the contribution figure.

The headroom to the 400 percent line is {formatMoney(fourHundred - exampleIncome)}. That is the
amount of additional MAGI that would remove the household from the credit range under the current
rules.

## What to do before you apply

Gather the tax return for the most recent year, pay stubs or a profit-and-loss statement for the
current year, and documents for any other income. Write down the household members and who claims
whom. Then use the official income page to check the MAGI items you might have missed.

If the estimate is near the 400 percent line, consider whether a deduction or a timing change is
available. Do not hide income to stay under the line, and do not assume a small overage will be
ignored. The application asks for a good-faith projection.

## A checklist for the estimate

Build the estimate in the same order as the tax return. Start with wages, self-employment income,
and retirement distributions. Add interest, dividends, capital gains, and rental income. Add the
items the Marketplace instructions require even when they are not taxable in the usual sense.

Then list the household members and confirm who is claimed on the return. Check the prior-year
poverty guideline for that household size. Compare the projected income with the 400 percent line.
If the result is close, write down the headroom and keep it with the application.

Finally, note the date of the estimate and the documents used. If the income changes during the
year, the note shows what was known when the application was filed. That is useful if the credit is
reconciled later.

## Marketplace MAGI vs taxable income

Marketplace MAGI and taxable income are related, but they are not the same figure. Taxable income
comes after deductions. MAGI starts with adjusted gross income, which is before the standard or
itemized deduction, and then adds certain items the Marketplace counts.

That difference can be large. A household that takes the standard deduction may have modest taxable
income and a much higher MAGI. A household with tax-exempt interest may have no tax on the interest
but still see it in the Marketplace calculation. A household with excluded foreign income may owe
no US tax on that income and still have to add it back for the credit test.

Above-the-line deductions are the ones that can help. A deductible traditional IRA contribution, a
health savings account contribution that meets the rules, and certain self-employed retirement
contributions can reduce AGI. A charitable deduction or a mortgage interest deduction generally
does not reduce MAGI because it comes later in the return.

Capital gains deserve special attention. A large gain can raise MAGI even if the tax rate on the
gain is low or zero. A Roth conversion can raise MAGI even though the conversion is a deliberate
retirement move. A withdrawal from a traditional IRA or 401(k) can raise MAGI unless it is a
qualified rollover.

The practical approach is to start with the expected tax return, not with a pay stub. Add the
Marketplace items, subtract the above-the-line deductions that apply, and compare the result with
the poverty guideline. Keep the worksheet with the application. If the household has self-
employment income, foreign income, or a one-time event, ask a tax preparer to check the MAGI
before the application is filed.

## Where the income estimate can go wrong

1. The poverty guideline year is not the coverage year. Using the wrong table can change the
   percentage and the credit.
2. This page does not calculate a premium tax credit because the benchmark premium is not in the
   data layer. It explains the income test and the limit.
3. The enhanced subsidy rules can change. Check the current law and the official Marketplace page
   before relying on any statement about the 400 percent line.
