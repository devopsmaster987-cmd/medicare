## What changed in the standard premium

The standard Part B premium is the amount a person pays before any income-related adjustment or late
enrollment penalty. For {year}, the standard monthly premium is {formatMoney(premium)}. For{' '}
{priorYear}, it was {formatMoney(priorPremium)}. The monthly change is{' '}
{formatMoney(monthlyIncrease)}, and the change across a full year is{' '}
{formatMoney(annualIncrease)}.

The deductible moved too. For {year}, the Part B deductible is {formatMoney(deductible)}, compared
with {formatMoney(priorDeductible)} in {priorYear}. The deductible is the amount you pay for covered
Part B services before Medicare begins to pay its share. After the deductible, the usual Part B
coinsurance is 20 percent of the Medicare-approved amount, and there is no annual out-of-pocket cap
in Original Medicare.

The premium and the deductible are different numbers with different jobs. A higher premium is a
monthly cost that continues all year. A higher deductible is a one-time threshold before cost
sharing begins. A household budget should account for both, plus the cost of any supplemental
coverage, Part D, or Medigap policy.

The standard premium is not a quote for everyone. It is the base amount. Two common adjustments can
raise an individual premium above it: the income-related monthly adjustment amount, usually called
IRMAA, and the Part B late enrollment penalty. The two adjustments can apply at the same time.

## How Medicare sets the Part B premium

Part B is financed through premiums and federal general revenue. The premium is designed to cover a
share of program spending, and the deductible and coinsurance are set alongside it. The Centers for
Medicare and Medicaid Services publishes the amounts, usually in the fall, for the following
calendar year.

The timing matters because Social Security cost-of-living adjustments are announced around the same
period. For people who have Part B premiums withheld from Social Security benefits, the net monthly
benefit depends on both numbers. A premium increase can absorb part or all of a cost-of-living
increase, even when the gross benefit rises.

Some beneficiaries are protected by the hold-harmless rule. In general, if a person has Part B
premiums withheld from Social Security and the premium increase would reduce the net benefit below
the prior year's amount, the premium increase may be limited for that year. The rule has exceptions,
including people who are new to Medicare, people who pay IRMAA, and people who are not receiving
Social Security benefits. The official notice is the reliable source for an individual case.

The standard premium is a national figure. It does not vary by state, by doctor, or by the Part B
services a person uses. What varies is the adjustment applied to the individual, and the amount of
care that falls outside Part B.

## Why your premium can be higher than the standard amount

IRMAA is based on modified adjusted gross income from two years earlier. A higher income can move a
person into a higher Part B premium tier. The surcharge is added to the standard premium, not
subtracted from it. A married couple can each pay the surcharge, so the household effect can be
larger than a single person's notice suggests.

The two-year lookback is the part that surprises people. Income from a Roth conversion, a large
capital gain, a business sale, or a retirement distribution can change a premium two years later.
A person who has a life-changing event, such as retirement or the death of a spouse, may be able to
ask Social Security to use newer income by filing Form SSA-44. The form does not guarantee a
reduction, and the accepted reasons and proof requirements matter.

The late enrollment penalty is separate from IRMAA. For Part B, the penalty is generally 10 percent
of the standard premium for each full 12-month period a person could have had Part B but did not
enroll, and the penalty is permanent. A person who delayed Part B without creditable employer
coverage can pay both the penalty and any IRMAA that applies.

Neither adjustment changes the deductible. A person with a higher premium still faces the same
standard Part B deductible, unless a separate rule applies. The premium notice shows the total
monthly amount, and the explanation should identify any IRMAA or penalty included.

## What the premium does not include

Part B covers physician services, outpatient care, durable medical equipment, and some preventive
services. It does not cover most prescription drugs, and it does not replace the hospital coverage
in Part A. A person usually needs a separate Part D plan for drug coverage, and may choose a Medigap
policy or another supplemental plan to help with cost sharing.

Part A is premium-free for most people who have 40 or more quarters of Medicare-covered work.
People with fewer quarters may pay a Part A premium. That premium is separate from the Part B
premium and is not included in the standard Part B amount shown here.

Part D has its own premium, deductible, and out-of-pocket cap. A Part D plan can also have an
IRMAA surcharge, which is billed separately from the plan premium in many cases. Medigap premiums
are set by the insurer and are not part of Medicare's standard premium.

Dental, vision, hearing, and long-term custodial care are generally not covered by Original
Medicare. A person who wants help with those costs may need a separate policy or another program.
The Part B premium should be budgeted alongside those choices, not treated as the full cost of
coverage.

## Worked example

Consider a person who pays the standard Part B premium for all twelve months and has no IRMAA or
late enrollment penalty. For {year}, the annual standard premium is {formatMoney(annualPremium)}.
For {priorYear}, the same calculation was {formatMoney(annualPriorPremium)}. The annual difference
is {formatMoney(annualIncrease)}.

Now add the deductible. The person pays the first {formatMoney(deductible)} of covered Part B
services before Medicare begins its share. After that, the person generally pays 20 percent of the
Medicare-approved amount for covered services. The premium does not count toward the deductible, and
the deductible does not reduce the premium.

If the same person had a Part B late enrollment penalty of one full year, the penalty would add 10
percent of the standard premium to the monthly amount. That is a permanent addition. The exact
dollar figure depends on the standard premium in each year, so the penalty can change when the
standard premium changes.

The example is deliberately simple. It shows the relationship between the monthly premium, the
annual cost, and the deductible. It does not estimate a person's total health care spending, because
that depends on the care used, the supplemental coverage chosen, and the prices charged by
providers.

## Why the standard premium is not your bill

1. The standard premium is not a personal quote. IRMAA, a late enrollment penalty, and the
   hold-harmless rule can change the amount actually withheld or billed.
2. The premium is set for a calendar year and can change again. A budget based on one year should be
   reviewed when the next official amount is published.
3. This page explains the standard figures and the common adjustments. It does not calculate an
   individual IRMAA tier or penalty. Use the official notice and the calculators for those numbers.

## Compare the notice with the standard amount

If you are already enrolled, compare the premium notice with the standard amount and check whether
the notice explains an IRMAA or penalty adjustment. If you are about to enroll, read the
[Part B late enrollment penalty calculator](/calculators/part-b-late-enrollment-penalty) before you
delay coverage, and use the [IRMAA cliff checker](/calculators/irmaa-cliff-checker) if your income
is near a threshold. The [Medicare vs ACA decision guide](/calculators/medicare-vs-aca) is the place
to start if you are still choosing between an employer plan and Medicare.
