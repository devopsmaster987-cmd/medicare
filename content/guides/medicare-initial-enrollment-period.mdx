## What the initial enrollment period is

The initial enrollment period is the first window most people have to sign up for Medicare Part A and Part B. It is built around the month a person turns 65. The window is seven months long: the three months before the birthday month, the birthday month itself, and the three months after.

The window is not a deadline for every person. Someone who is still working and has qualifying employer coverage may be able to delay Part B without a penalty. Someone who is not working and does not have qualifying coverage should treat the window as the main enrollment opportunity.

Part A and Part B are the core of Original Medicare. Part A is hospital insurance, and Part B is medical insurance. A person who wants drug coverage also needs a Part D plan or a Medicare Advantage plan with drug coverage. The initial enrollment period is the time to make those choices together, not one at a time.

The date that matters is the month a person turns 65, not the exact birthday. A person born in the middle of a month still uses the birthday month as the anchor. A person born on the first day of a month is treated as reaching 65 in the prior month for Medicare purposes, which can shift the window.

## The seven-month window in practice

Suppose a person turns 65 in {exampleBirthdayMonth}. The window normally begins in {exampleWindowStart} and ends in {exampleWindowEnd}. If the person enrolls during the three months before the birthday month, coverage can begin on the first day of the birthday month.

If the person enrolls during the birthday month, coverage generally begins the first day of the following month. If the person enrolls during the three months after the birthday month, coverage begins the first day of the month after enrollment, and the start can be later than the birthday month.

The timing is not only about the start date. A late enrollment can create a gap in coverage and, for Part B, a late enrollment penalty. The penalty is generally permanent. A person who is unsure about the exact dates should use the official Medicare tools or contact Social Security before the window closes.

The first-of-month birthday rule is easy to miss. If a birthday falls on the first day of a month, the person is considered to turn 65 in the previous month. That can move the entire window one month earlier. A person born on the first day of {exampleBirthdayMonth}, for example, may need to use the prior month as the anchor.

## What to enroll in during the window

Most people should enroll in Part A and Part B during the initial enrollment period. Part A is premium-free for people with 40 or more work quarters, and Part B has a monthly premium. A person who is not eligible for premium-free Part A may need to decide whether to buy Part A.

Drug coverage is a separate decision. A person who has no creditable prescription drug coverage should consider a Part D plan during the initial enrollment period. Waiting can create a Part D late enrollment penalty. A person with creditable employer or retiree coverage may be able to delay Part D without a penalty.

A Medigap policy is also a separate choice. The Medigap open enrollment period generally begins when Part B starts and lasts six months. During that period, an insurer generally cannot refuse to sell a policy or charge more because of health status, although the rules vary by state. Missing that window can make a policy harder or more expensive to buy later.

A person who chooses a Medicare Advantage plan instead of Original Medicare should compare the plan's network, drug coverage, and cost sharing. This page is about the enrollment window, not about which type of plan is better. The decision should be based on the person's doctors, drugs, travel, and budget.

## If you are still working

A person who is still working may be able to delay Part B if the employer coverage is based on current employment and the employer has {employerSize} or more employees. In that situation, the employer plan generally pays first, and Medicare may be secondary. The person can usually enroll in Part B later through a special enrollment period without a penalty.

The rule is different for a small employer. If the employer has fewer than {employerSize} employees, Medicare generally pays first, and the person should usually enroll in Part B during the initial enrollment period. Delaying in that situation can create a penalty and a gap.

Coverage based on a spouse's current employment can also qualify. A person who is covered through a working spouse's employer plan should check the employer size and the plan's creditable status. Retiree coverage and COBRA are generally not treated the same as current employer coverage for the delay rules.

The employer's human resources office is the best source for the plan's status. A person should ask whether the coverage is creditable for Part D and whether the employer has {employerSize} or more employees. The answers should be in writing, because the enrollment decision may depend on them.

## What happens if you miss the window

A person who misses the initial enrollment period may be able to enroll during the General Enrollment Period, which runs from January 1 through March 31 each year. Coverage may not start immediately, and a Part B late enrollment penalty may apply. The penalty is generally permanent.

The Part B penalty is based on the standard premium and the number of full 12-month periods without Part B. A person who had creditable employer coverage during the gap may be able to avoid the penalty. A person who simply missed the window usually cannot.

The Part D penalty is separate. It is based on the national base beneficiary premium and the number of full months without creditable drug coverage. A person who missed the drug coverage window can owe a penalty even if Part B was handled correctly.

A coverage gap can be expensive. A person who needs care during the gap may pay the full cost or may have limited options. The safest approach is to enroll on time or to confirm in writing that a delay is allowed.

## Worked example

Consider a person who turns 65 in {exampleBirthdayMonth} and has no employer coverage. The initial enrollment period normally runs from {exampleWindowStart} through{' '}
{exampleWindowEnd}. The person enrolls in {exampleWindowStart}. Because enrollment happened before the birthday month, Part A and Part B can begin on the first day of{' '}
{exampleBirthdayMonth}.

A second person waits until {exampleWindowEnd} to enroll. Coverage may begin later, and the person may have a gap. If the delay includes a full 12-month period without Part B and no creditable coverage, a Part B late enrollment penalty may apply.

A third person is still working for an employer with {employerSize} or more employees and has creditable coverage. That person may be able to delay Part B and enroll later through a special enrollment period. The employer coverage and the employment status are the key facts.

The examples use the same birthday month but different enrollment dates. The difference in outcome comes from the timing and the coverage available. A person with employer coverage should not automatically delay, and a person without it should not automatically wait.

## Three honest caveats

1. The window is based on the birthday month, and the first-of-month rule can shift it. Confirm the dates for your own birthday.
2. Employer coverage does not always justify a delay. The employer size, the employment status, and the creditable status all matter.
3. Missing the window can create both a penalty and a coverage gap. The cost of waiting is usually higher than the cost of checking.
