## When should I sign up for Medicare at 65

Sign up during the {months}-month initial enrollment period: the {monthsBefore} months before your
birthday month, your birthday month, and the {monthsAfter} months after it. The month you turn 65 is
the anchor, not the exact day of the month. Enrolling during the {monthsBefore} months before the
birthday month is usually the safest choice, because coverage can begin on the first day of your
birthday month with no gap.

The exact months depend on your birthday month. A person with a June birthday has a window that
runs from March through September. A person born on the first day of a month is treated as turning
65 in the previous month for Medicare purposes, which moves the whole window one month earlier, so
the dates should be confirmed with Social Security before relying on them.

Signing up early in the window matters for more reason than one. The Medicare card, the Part D plan
choice, and any Medigap application all run better with time in hand. A late sign-up can create a
gap where you owe the full cost of care, and for Part B it can create a penalty that is permanent.

## Do you automatically get enrolled in Medicare at 65

Automatic enrollment depends on your Social Security status. People who already receive Social
Security retirement benefits are generally enrolled in Part A and Part B without filing anything,
and the Medicare card arrives by mail a few months before coverage starts. The same automatic path
applies to people receiving Social Security disability benefits after the required waiting period.

People who have not claimed Social Security are a different case. If you plan to keep working, delay
the claim, or take benefits later, there is no automatic enrollment. You must apply during the
initial enrollment period, usually online at the Social Security website. Missing the window is the
mistake that produces coverage gaps and penalties, because nothing arrives in the mail to remind
you when no benefit check exists.

The split matters because automatic enrollment can be a trap of its own. Someone receiving Social
Security at 65 is enrolled in Part B automatically and will start paying the{' '}
{formatMoney(premium)} premium, even if an employer plan was going to be primary. A person in that
situation who wants to rely on the employer plan needs to contact Social Security and decline Part
B before the coverage starts, which is a call that should be made in the first {monthsBefore}{' '}
months before the birthday month.

## The checklist, step by step

1. Confirm your enrollment window from your birthday month, and check the first-of-month rule.
2. Ask your employer whether the plan counts as coverage based on current employment, and whether
   the employer has {employerSize} or more employees, in writing.
3. If you or a spouse claimed Social Security already, check whether enrollment is automatic and
   whether Part B should be declined in favor of the employer plan.
4. Stop HSA contributions at least {hsaStopMonths} months before any Part A start date, because
   Part A can be backdated up to six months and a later contribution becomes an excess
   contribution.
5. Compare the standard Part B premium of {formatMoney(premium)} with what you pay for the employer
   plan now, including the employer share that disappears at retirement.
6. Choose drug coverage in the same window. A gap in creditable drug coverage of 63 days or more can
   create a Part D penalty that is added to every future plan premium.
7. If you take Original Medicare, look at Medigap during the six-month open enrollment that begins
   when Part B starts, when insurers generally must sell you a policy without medical
   underwriting.
8. Confirm the Part A and Part B start dates in writing before you cancel any current coverage.

## What turning 65 costs in {year}

Part A is premium-free for people with 40 or more work quarters, and most people qualify through
their own record or a spouse's record. A person with 30 to 39 quarters pays{' '}
{formatMoney(partAReduced)} a month for Part A, and a person with fewer than 30 quarters pays{' '}
{formatMoney(partAFull)} a month. Part B is never premium-free: the standard premium is{' '}
{formatMoney(premium)} a month for {year}, and the deductible is {formatMoney(deductible)} for the
year.

Two adjustments can raise the Part B premium above the standard amount. The income-related
surcharge applies when modified adjusted gross income two years earlier was above the first
threshold, and the late enrollment penalty applies when a person delayed Part B without creditable
coverage. A household budget for turning 65 should use the premium notice, not the standard figure,
whenever either adjustment could apply.

## If you are still working at 65

Employer coverage based on current employment can justify delaying Part B. The employer size test
is the {employerSize}-employee rule: at an employer with {employerSize} or more employees, the
group plan generally pays first for an active worker, and Part B can be enrolled later without a
penalty through the eight-month special enrollment period. Under {employerSize} employees, Medicare
generally pays first, and delaying Part B can create both a coverage gap and a permanent penalty.

COBRA and retiree coverage are not the same thing in this test. Neither counts as coverage based on
current employment, so relying on either as a substitute for Part B is a common path to the late
enrollment penalty. The exception is an employer of any size that is large enough to be exempt
from COBRA, and the details should be confirmed with the plan administrator.

The special enrollment period runs for {sepMonths} months after employment or coverage ends,
whichever comes first. Enrollment within that window avoids the penalty. A person who wants
Medicare to start as employer coverage ends should enroll the month before the ending month, so the
start dates line up without a gap.

## Worked example

A person turns 65 in September {year} and works at an employer with 300 employees. The initial
enrollment period runs from June through December. The person stays on the employer plan, enrolls
in premium-free Part A during the window, and delays Part B. Two years later, the person retires in
March. The eight-month special enrollment period runs from March, so Part B is enrolled in
September, with coverage starting in October, one month after the enrollment paperwork. No penalty
applies because the employer coverage was based on current employment at an employer above the
{employerSize}-employee threshold.

A second person turns 65 the same month at a firm with 12 employees. The employer plan is below the
{employerSize}-employee mark, so Medicare pays first. The person enrolls in Part A and Part B during
the initial window, pays the {formatMoney(premium)} standard premium from October onward, and keeps
the employer plan as secondary coverage. Because the standard premium is less than the cost of most
individual plans, the person keeps both, and Part B fills the gaps the small-group plan leaves.

A third person turns 65, does nothing, and retires the following year without employer coverage.
The person enrolls the next January during the general enrollment period. Coverage starts in
February, after a one-month wait, and every full 12 months of delay adds 10 percent of the standard
premium to the bill for life. The three examples turn on the same questions: employer size,
employment status, and the window dates.

## What to gather before you apply

1. The Social Security number, birth certificate, and current address for the application.
2. The employer plan documents, including the creditable-coverage notice, if you plan to delay
   Part B.
3. The list of current prescriptions, dosages, and pharmacies, for comparing Part D plans.
4. The latest tax return, because the income surcharge is based on modified adjusted gross income
   from two years earlier.

## What the {months}-month window does not decide

1. The window sets when you can enroll, not what you pay. The premium notice after enrollment is
   the binding figure, because an income surcharge or penalty can change it.
2. Delaying is only safe with qualifying employer coverage. A person without it should treat the
   initial enrollment period as the deadline, not a suggestion.
3. This page is about timing and enrollment, not plan choice. Original Medicare with Medigap and a
   Medicare Advantage plan are different paths, and the choice should be made against your own
   doctors, drugs, and budget.

## Where to check the date before you apply

The figures on this page come from the CMS {year} premium fact sheet and Medicare.gov enrollment
pages, checked on {dataVerifiedOnText}. The Social Security office handles the application, and the
state SHIP program offers free counseling for the surrounding choices. Before you enroll, use the
[Part B late enrollment penalty calculator](/calculators/part-b-late-enrollment-penalty/) to price a
delayed sign-up, the [employer coverage affordability calculator](/calculators/employer-coverage-affordability/)
to test the employer plan, and the [IRMAA cliff checker](/calculators/irmaa-cliff-checker/) if your
income is near a surcharge threshold. The September-to-December Medicare open enrollment window is
a separate yearly window that changes existing coverage after you are enrolled.
