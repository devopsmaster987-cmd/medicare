## Who qualifies before 65

Medicare is often described as a program for people 65 and older, but that is only one path. A person under 65 can qualify through Social Security disability benefits, end-stage renal disease, or ALS.

The most common path is Social Security Disability Insurance, or SSDI. A person who is approved for SSDI generally becomes eligible for Medicare after 24 months of benefits. The 24-month period is a waiting period, not a work requirement.

The wait can feel long, especially for a person who needs ongoing care. During the wait, the person may have Medicaid, employer coverage, marketplace coverage, or another source of insurance. The person should plan for the gap rather than assume Medicare starts immediately.

A person with ALS is an exception. Medicare can begin when disability benefits begin, without the usual 24-month wait. A person with end-stage renal disease has a separate path with its own rules.

## The 24-month waiting period

The 24-month period generally starts when the person becomes entitled to SSDI benefits, not when the application is filed. The date on the award letter matters. A person who is approved after a long appeal may have a retroactive entitlement date.

Medicare generally begins in the 25th month of entitlement. A person who is not sure about the date should check the award letter and contact Social Security. The enrollment window is based on that date.

The waiting period can be shorter for some people. ALS is the clearest example. A person with a different condition should ask Social Security whether an exception applies.

A person who is working and has employer coverage during the wait should check how that coverage interacts with Medicare later. The employer size and the coverage type can affect the coordination rules.

## The initial enrollment period

A person who qualifies for Medicare through disability gets an initial enrollment period. The window is generally seven months, based around the Medicare eligibility date. The person can enroll before the eligibility date, during the month, or in the months after.

The enrollment package may arrive automatically, but a person should not rely on it. The person should confirm the dates and the parts of Medicare being offered. Part A and Part B are separate decisions, although most people take both.

A person who does not enroll during the initial period may have to wait for a general enrollment period. That can create a gap and a late enrollment penalty. The penalty rules are the same as for people who qualify at 65.

A person who has employer coverage may have a special enrollment period. The employer size and the coverage type matter. The person should check before delaying.

## Working while on disability

A person who returns to work may be able to keep Medicare for a time. The rules are different from the age-65 rules, and the person should check the Social Security work incentives.

Ticket to Work is a program that can help a person return to work while keeping benefits for a period. The details depend on the person's earnings and the program rules. The person should ask Social Security before making a change.

A person who loses SSDI eligibility may later qualify for Medicare at 65. The work record and the disability history can matter. The person should keep the award letters and the Medicare notices.

A person who is working and has employer coverage should check whether the employer plan is primary or secondary. The 20-employee rule can apply, but the disability path has its own coordination rules. The person should ask both the plan and Medicare.

## Drug coverage and extra help

A person with Medicare through disability may also need Part D. The enrollment rules and the late enrollment penalty apply. A person who has creditable drug coverage may be able to delay Part D without a penalty.

A person with limited income and resources may qualify for Extra Help with Part D costs. The person may also qualify for a Medicare Savings Program through the state Medicaid agency. The eligibility rules vary by state.

A person who is dual eligible for Medicare and Medicaid may have most costs covered. The coordination between the two programs can be complicated. A person should ask the state Medicaid agency and the Part D plan how the coverage works.

A person who is not sure about the drug coverage should check the plan's formulary and the pharmacy network. The lowest premium is not always the lowest total cost.

## Worked example

Suppose a person is approved for SSDI and becomes entitled to benefits in January. The 24-month waiting period runs, and Medicare generally begins in the 25th month. The person receives an enrollment package and confirms the dates.

The person enrolls in Part A and Part B during the initial enrollment period. The person also checks whether the employer coverage from a spouse is primary or secondary. The plan documents explain the coordination.

A second person with ALS becomes entitled to disability benefits and qualifies for Medicare sooner. The person enrolls when the benefits begin, without waiting 24 months.

A third person returns to work and keeps Medicare for a period under the work incentives. The person checks the rules with Social Security before changing coverage.

The examples show that the disability path has its own timing. The date on the award letter is the starting point for the enrollment decision.

## Common mistakes

One mistake is counting the 24-month period from the application date instead of the entitlement date. The award letter is the source for the start date. A person who uses the wrong date may miss the enrollment window.

Another mistake is assuming the 24-month rule applies to everyone. ALS and ESRD have different paths. The person should ask Social Security which rule applies.

A third mistake is ignoring employer coverage coordination. A person who is still working may have a plan that pays first or second. The plan documents explain the order. A written answer from the plan is more reliable than a phone summary. Keep it with the enrollment records and review it if a claim is denied. Do not rely on memory.

## Three honest caveats

1. The 24-month waiting period can create a coverage gap. Plan for it before the wait begins.
2. The ALS path is different. Do not assume the 24-month rule applies to everyone.
3. Work incentives and employer coverage can change the coordination. Check with Social Security and the plan.

## What to do next

Find the entitlement date on your Social Security award letter. That date starts the clock for the initial enrollment period. If you have employer coverage, check the coordination rules before delaying. The
[Medicare and ALS guide](/guides/medicare-and-als) explains the faster path, and the
[Medicare and end stage renal disease guide](/guides/medicare-and-end-stage-renal-disease) covers the kidney path. If you need help with costs, use the
[Medicare Savings Program calculator](/calculators/medicare-savings-program-extra-help).
