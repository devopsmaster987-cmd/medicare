## The separate-filer table

Married filing separately does not share the joint table. It has its own thresholds, and the first
surcharge starts at {formatMoney(separateFirst.min)}. That is far below the joint first threshold of{' '}
{formatMoney(partB2026.irmaa.marriedJoint[1].min)}.

The difference is intentional. The tax filing status is treated differently for IRMAA, so a couple
that files separately can reach a surcharge at a lower income than a couple that files jointly. The
table has fewer tiers, and the jump from the standard tier to the first surcharge is large.

The top separate-filer tier begins at {formatMoney(separateSecond.min)}. At that level the Part B
total is {formatMoney(separateSecond.partBTotal)} and the Part D add-on is{' '}
{formatMoney(separateSecond.partDAdjustment)}.

## Why the threshold is lower

The separate-filer table reflects the tax rules for that filing status. It is not a mistake in the
calculator, and it is not a penalty invented by Medicare. The thresholds are published with the
other IRMAA tiers and apply to each person who files separately.

A couple may file separately for many reasons, including a legal separation, a disagreement about
finances, or advice from a tax preparer. Whatever the reason, the Medicare premium follows the
filing status on the return that SSA receives.

If the filing status changes, the IRMAA table changes with it. That is why a couple should check the
status on the lookback return, not just the status they plan to use this year.

## How each spouse is billed

Each spouse has a separate Social Security record and a separate Medicare premium notice. If both
spouses are enrolled in Medicare, each can receive an IRMAA decision based on that spouse's income.
The household total can be the sum of two separate surcharges.

Do not double one spouse's number unless both spouses have the same income and the same tier. Read
each notice. The amounts can differ because the incomes differ, the filing status applies to each
return, and the Part D coverage can differ.

If only one spouse is enrolled in Medicare, only that spouse's premium is affected. The other
spouse's income may still matter for the tax return, but it does not create a Medicare premium for
someone who is not enrolled.

## What to check on the notice

Check the income figure, the tax year it came from, the filing status, and the tier. Then compare the
Part B amount with the table. If the notice uses joint thresholds for a separate return, or the
wrong income year, ask Social Security to review it.

Check the Part D line separately. The Part D IRMAA amount is added to the plan premium and can be
billed in a different way. A zero Part D amount does not mean the Part B surcharge is zero.

Keep the notice with the tax return for the lookback year. If you later file an SSA-44 or ask for a
review, the notice is the document that shows what SSA decided.

## Can an SSA-44 help

Form SSA-44 can help if a listed life-changing event changed the income used for the decision. The
form is not limited to joint filers. A marriage, divorce, death of a spouse, retirement, or another
listed event can support a request to use a newer income figure.

The proof still has to match the event. A separate return does not by itself prove that income
changed. The event and the documents do. Use the official form and ask SSA what it needs for the
specific situation.

## Worked example

A married person files separately and reports {formatMoney(exampleMagi)} of modified adjusted gross
income in the lookback year. That is above the first separate-filer threshold of{' '}
{formatMoney(separateFirst.min)}, so the first surcharge tier applies.

The Part B total is {formatMoney(separateFirst.partBTotal)}, and the Part D add-on is{' '}
{formatMoney(separateFirst.partDAdjustment)}. The combined monthly surcharge is{' '}
{formatMoney(firstSurcharge)}, or {formatMoney(firstAnnual)} a year. The standard premium for the
same year is {formatMoney(standardMonthly)} a month. The difference is{' '}
{formatMoney(firstSurcharge)} a month.

If the spouse also has Medicare and a similar income, the household can face a second surcharge.
That is why the household view matters even when the returns are separate.

## What a joint return would have shown

A joint return uses the joint table. The first surcharge starts at {formatMoney(partB2026.irmaa.marriedJoint[1].min)},
which is much higher than the separate-filer line. The same combined income can therefore produce a
standard premium on a joint return and a surcharge on separate returns.

That comparison is not a reason to choose a filing status. The tax return is a legal document, and
the filing status affects deductions, credits, and liability. Medicare is one factor among many. Use
a tax preparer to compare the whole picture.

The comparison is useful for understanding the notice. If a couple files separately and one spouse
sees a surcharge at an income that looks modest, the separate table explains why. The number is not
a mistake in the calculator or the notice.

## Separate returns and the rest of Medicare

IRMAA is not the only Medicare rule that looks at income. Extra Help and the Medicare Savings
Programs use different income and resource tests. A separate return can affect those applications
too, but the rules are not the same as the IRMAA table.

Do not assume that a low IRMAA tier means a household will qualify for Extra Help. The programs have
their own limits and their own application process. Use the
[Medicare Savings Program and Extra Help](/calculators/medicare-savings-program-extra-help)
calculator for that question.

The Part B premium itself is separate from the tax return. A person who files separately still pays
the standard premium if the income is below the separate-filer line. The filing status changes the
line, not the basic premium.

## A checklist before you file

Confirm the filing status on the lookback return. Confirm the income figure SSA used. Compare the
notice with the separate-filer table. Check each spouse’s notice separately. If a life-changing
event applies, gather the proof before filing Form SSA-44.

Keep the tax return and the Medicare notices together. If the filing status changes in a later year,
the new return will drive a later premium. A change is not retroactive to an earlier notice unless
SSA reviews it.

If the numbers do not match, ask Social Security for the calculation in writing. A short letter that
shows the income, the year, the filing status, and the table is easier to review than a phone call.

## Married filing separately IRMAA thresholds

The married-filing-separately table is not a smaller version of the joint table. It has its own
lower thresholds, and the first surcharge can begin at an income level that would be unremarkable
on a joint return. That is why a couple who files separately can be surprised by a Part B or Part D
surcharge even when the household's combined income looks moderate.

Each spouse is treated as an individual for the surcharge. If both are enrolled in Medicare, each
can receive a notice and each can owe the add-on. The household total can therefore be twice the
single amount shown on one notice. Check both notices rather than assuming one covers the couple.

A change in filing status can change the tier. A marriage, divorce, or death of a spouse can move a
person from one table to another. Form SSA-44 is built around listed life-changing events, and a
filing-status change may be relevant. The form still requires proof and a newer income figure. It
is not an automatic reset.

The separate-filer rules can also affect other parts of the tax return. A couple who files
separately may lose certain credits or deductions. The IRMAA table is one cost to weigh, not the
only one. A tax preparer should see the whole return before the couple decides that separate filing
is worth it.

If a notice looks wrong, compare the income figure with the return that was actually filed. If the
figure is right and no listed event applies, the practical route is to plan the next income year.
If the figure is wrong, ask Social Security to correct the record and keep the documentation.

## Three honest caveats

1. The separate-filer thresholds are much lower than the joint thresholds. A couple that files
   separately can owe a surcharge at an income that would be in the standard tier on a joint return.
2. Each spouse can be billed separately. Do not assume the household cost is one surcharge.
3. This page is guidance, not tax advice. The filing status on the return drives the table, so
   confirm the status with a tax preparer before changing it for Medicare reasons.
