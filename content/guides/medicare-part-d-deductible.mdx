## What the Part D deductible is

The Part D deductible is the amount you pay for covered prescription drugs before your plan begins
to share the cost. For {year}, no Medicare drug plan may charge a deductible higher than{' '}
{formatMoney(deductible)}. Some plans charge less, and some charge no deductible at all. The
deductible applies to the plan's covered drug list, so a drug that is not on the list does not
count toward it.

The deductible resets each calendar year. A person who meets it in January does not owe it again
until January of the next year. A person who joins a plan mid-year owes the deductible for the
remainder of that year, and it resets with the new plan year.

## Medicare Part D premium {year}

The Part D premium is what you pay each month for the drug plan. It varies by plan and by region,
and it is separate from the Part B premium. The national base beneficiary premium, which is the
figure Medicare uses for penalty and subsidy calculations, is {formatMoney(basePremium)} a month
for {year}. A person's actual plan premium can be higher or lower than that.

About two-thirds of beneficiaries pay the premium directly to the plan, and the rest have it
deducted from their Social Security benefit. A person with a higher income can owe an
income-related surcharge on top of the plan premium, and that surcharge is billed separately from
the plan premium in many cases. The surcharge runs from 0 dollars up to 91 dollars a month for {year}.

## Medicare Part D cost {year}

The total cost of Part D for a person depends on four things: the plan premium, the deductible, the
cost sharing on each prescription, and whether the out-of-pocket cap is reached. Cost sharing in
the middle phase is 25 percent of the cost of covered drugs, both generic and brand-name, until
out-of-pocket spending reaches {formatMoney(cap)}. At that point, catastrophic coverage begins and
covered drugs cost nothing for the rest of the year.

The cap counts the deductible, the coinsurance and copayments, and certain payments made on the
person's behalf, such as through the Extra Help program. It does not count the monthly premium. A
person who pays a high premium but uses few drugs can still spend less in total than a person with
a low premium and heavy drug use, which is why the plan comparison should be based on the actual
prescription list.

## What counts toward the cap

Payments that count toward the {formatMoney(cap)} cap include the deductible, coinsurance,
copayments for covered drugs, and certain payments made on the person's behalf. Payments that do
not count include the monthly premium, drugs not on the plan's formulary, and drugs bought outside
the plan's network or without using the plan's card. A person who fills a prescription at a
non-preferred pharmacy or buys a drug the plan does not cover can spend money that does not move
them closer to the cap.

The cap is per person and per calendar year. A married couple each has their own cap, and unused
cap does not carry over. A person who reaches the cap in November pays nothing for covered drugs
in December, and the counter resets in January.

## How Extra Help changes the numbers

Extra Help, also called the Low-Income Subsidy, pays the Part D premium up to a benchmark amount,
the deductible, and most of the cost sharing. A person who qualifies for Extra Help pays a small
copayment per prescription instead of the 25 percent coinsurance, and pays nothing once the
catastrophic threshold is reached. Income and resource limits apply, and a person who qualifies
for a Medicare Savings Program or Supplemental Security Income qualifies automatically.

The limits and copayment amounts change each year. For {year}, the income limit is{' '}
{formatMoney(23940)} for an individual and {formatMoney(32460)} for a married couple, with resource
limits of {formatMoney(18090)} and {formatMoney(36100)}. The copayments are a few dollars per
prescription for people who qualify, and 0 dollars for people with full Medicaid in the Qualified
Medicare Beneficiary program.

## How to choose a Part D plan

A person choosing a Part D plan should compare three things: the premium, the deductible, and the
cost sharing on the specific drugs the person takes. A plan with a low premium and a high deductible
can cost more overall than a plan with a higher premium and no deductible, if the person takes
expensive drugs early in the year. The plan finder on Medicare.gov compares the total annual cost
for a given prescription list, which is more useful than comparing premiums alone.

The pharmacy network matters too. A plan can have a lower cost at a preferred pharmacy and a higher
cost at a standard one. A person who has a regular pharmacy should check whether it is preferred
before enrolling, and whether mail order is available for maintenance medications.

## Worked example

Consider a person with a plan that has the full {formatMoney(deductible)} deductible and a 25
percent coinsurance. In January, the person fills a prescription with a 400 dollars cost. The person pays
the first {formatMoney(deductible)} of that, which is the deductible, and 25 percent of the
remaining 215 dollars, which is 53.75 dollars. The person's out-of-pocket spending for January is{' '}
{formatMoney(deductible)} plus 53.75 dollars.

By October, the person's out-of-pocket spending has reached {formatMoney(cap)}. From that point, the
person pays nothing more for covered drugs for the rest of the year. The plan and Medicare pay the
rest. The monthly premium continued to be owed throughout the year, because the premium does not
count toward the cap.

A second person has Extra Help and the same prescription. The deductible is paid by the program, and
the person pays a small copayment per prescription instead of the 25 percent coinsurance. The
person reaches the catastrophic threshold faster, because payments made on the person's behalf
count toward the cap, and pays nothing for covered drugs for the rest of the year.

## What the Part D deductible does not decide

1. The deductible is a plan feature within a federal maximum. A plan with no deductible usually
   charges a higher premium, so the total cost depends on how the plan is structured.
2. The cap applies to covered drugs only. Drugs not on the formulary, or bought outside the plan,
   do not count toward it.
3. The premium is separate from the cap. A person can reach the cap and still owe the monthly
   premium.
4. This page explains the standard figures. It does not recommend a specific plan, because the
   right plan depends on the person's prescriptions, pharmacy, and budget.
