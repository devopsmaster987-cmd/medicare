## Why ALS is treated differently

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, or ALS, is a progressive disease that affects nerve cells. The Social Security rules treat ALS differently from many other disabilities. The usual waiting periods are generally waived, so a person with ALS can get Medicare sooner.

The 24-month waiting period that applies to many SSDI recipients generally does not apply to ALS. A person who is approved for disability benefits because of ALS can become eligible for Medicare when the benefits begin.

The faster path does not mean automatic enrollment. The person still needs to apply for Social Security disability benefits and provide the medical evidence. The Medicare enrollment follows the disability approval.

A person who is diagnosed with ALS should start the application process early. The paperwork and the medical records can take time, and the coverage start date depends on the approval.

## When coverage can start

A person with ALS generally becomes eligible for Medicare when Social Security disability benefits begin. The award letter should show the start date. The Medicare enrollment window is based on that date.

A person who is already receiving Social Security retirement benefits may have a different path. The person should ask Social Security which rule applies. The answer can depend on age and work history.

A person who is still working and has employer coverage should check how the coverage coordinates. The employer size and the plan type matter. A person with current employer coverage may be able to delay Part B, but the ALS path may make earlier enrollment more attractive.

A person who is not sure about the date should contact Social Security. The agency can explain the entitlement date and the enrollment window. A short call can prevent a gap.

## The initial enrollment period

A person with ALS gets an initial enrollment period based on the Medicare eligibility date. The window is generally seven months. The person can enroll before the eligibility date, during the month, or in the months after.

The enrollment package may arrive automatically, but the person should confirm the dates. A person who does not receive the package should contact Social Security. The package explains Part A and Part B.

A person who misses the initial enrollment period may face a delay and a late enrollment penalty. The penalty rules are the same as for other Medicare paths. The person should not assume the ALS exception also removes the penalty.

A person who has employer coverage may have a special enrollment period. The person should check the rules before delaying. The employer letter and the plan documents can support the application.

## Part A, Part B, and equipment

Part A covers inpatient hospital care. A person with ALS may need hospital care, rehabilitation, or skilled nursing care. Part A can help with those costs, subject to the deductible and coinsurance.

Part B covers outpatient care, durable medical equipment, and many other services. A person with ALS may need wheelchairs, ventilators, or other equipment. The person should check whether the equipment supplier accepts Medicare and whether the item is covered.

Part B also covers some therapy services. The rules and the limits can change, so the person should check the current coverage. The doctor's order and the documentation matter.

Part D covers prescription drugs. A person with ALS may need drugs that are on a specialty tier. The person should compare formularies and check whether the drugs are covered. The lowest premium plan may not cover the needed drugs.

## Employer coverage and coordination

A person with ALS who is still working may have employer coverage. The employer size determines whether the plan or Medicare pays first. A plan from an employer with 20 or more employees generally pays first for a current employee who is 65 or older.

A person who is under 65 and has employer coverage should check the plan's disability rules. The plan may coordinate differently, and the person should confirm the order of payment.

A person who keeps employer coverage should show both cards to providers. The provider should bill the correct payer first. A claim sent to the wrong payer can be delayed.

A person who loses employer coverage may have a special enrollment period. The person should apply promptly and keep the coverage records.

## Worked example

Suppose a person is diagnosed with ALS and applies for Social Security disability benefits. The application is approved, and the benefits begin in June. Medicare generally becomes available when the benefits begin, without the 24-month wait.

The person enrolls in Part A and Part B during the initial enrollment period. The person checks whether the employer coverage from a spouse is primary or secondary. The plan documents explain the coordination.

A second person with ALS delays the application because of paperwork. The delay pushes back the benefit start date and the Medicare start date. The person later wishes the application had been filed sooner.

A third person with ALS has 40 quarters and pays no Part A premium. The person still pays the Part B premium and compares Part D plans for the needed drugs.

The examples show that speed matters. The ALS exception removes a long wait, but it does not remove the need to apply and enroll.

## Common mistakes

One mistake is waiting for a 24-month period that does not apply. A person with ALS should ask Social Security about the faster path instead of assuming the standard wait.

Another mistake is delaying the disability application because the paperwork feels overwhelming. The application date can affect the benefit start date and the Medicare start date. Early help from a social worker or advocate can make the process easier.

A third mistake is assuming equipment is covered without checking. Part B covers many items, but the supplier and the documentation matter. The person should confirm before ordering.

A fourth mistake is skipping Part D because the drug costs seem manageable at first. A specialty drug can change the budget quickly. Comparing formularies early can prevent a costly surprise. Keep a list of the current drugs and check it against the plan's formulary before each open season. The list can also help the prescriber respond faster if an exception is needed.

## Three honest caveats

1. The ALS exception removes the 24-month wait, but it does not remove the Part B premium or the Part D rules.
2. The person still needs a Social Security disability approval. The medical evidence matters.
3. Equipment and drug coverage depend on the plan and the supplier. Check before ordering.

## What to do next

Start the Social Security disability application as soon as the diagnosis is confirmed. Keep the medical records and the award letter. The
[Medicare for people with disabilities guide](/guides/medicare-for-people-with-disabilities) explains the standard path, and the
[Medicare and end stage renal disease guide](/guides/medicare-and-end-stage-renal-disease) covers another exception. If you need help with premiums, use the
[Medicare Savings Program calculator](/calculators/medicare-savings-program-extra-help).
